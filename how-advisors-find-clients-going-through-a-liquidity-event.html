How Advisors Find Clients Going Through A Liquidity Event

By the time a business sale is announced, most of the planning is supposed to be finished already, and owners say they started it late. So I'd use a public event to choose who to know, and aim at the decisions that are still ahead.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Finding the event is the easy half. Many business sales are announced, and a senior executive's sale of company stock shows up in a public filing.
  • Owners start preparing late. In a 2023 UBS survey of U.S. business owners, 70% of those who had sold in recent years spent less than two years preparing, and eight in 10 wished they had started earlier.
  • Owners who are looking to exit do turn to financial advisors. In the same survey, 66% of owners who were looking to exit said they turned to financial advisors for advice.
  • No study I've found measures when the advisor gets chosen. So this part is my judgment: use the public event to choose who to get to know, start as a peer with no ask, and aim at the next decision.

Liquidity events aren't hard to find. Many business sales get announced. When a senior executive sells a meaningful block of company stock, it shows up in a public filing. An inheritance is quieter, but the family's estate attorney and CPA know about it. Any advisor who looks can see most of this, which is the trouble with treating the event as the opportunity.

There is a second trouble, and it's the one the research speaks to. For a business owner, the announcement comes at the end of the work. The tax and wealth transfer planning that deal professionals say can add to an owner's net proceeds has to happen before the closing, and owners report that they start preparing late.

The evidence here is thinner than on most questions I write about. The UBS and PNC surveys asked business owners, with no wealth threshold, and both firms sell advice to owners. The BNY Wealth report asked attorneys, investment bankers and CPAs about their clients, so it is their view of owners and not the owners' own account, and BNY sells wealth services too. The Long Angle poll is small and comes from a membership community. None of them measures when an owner hires a financial advisor relative to the sale, whether that advisor was new or already in place, or how often an advisor wins a client at or after a sale. I haven't found a study that does, so the strategy further down is my judgment.

Owners start preparing late, and most wish they had started sooner

UBS surveyed more than 500 U.S. business owners in April 2023, each with at least one employee and $1 million in annual revenue. Of those who had sold in recent years, 70% said they spent less than two years preparing for the exit, and eight in 10 wished they had started earlier. Among owners who weren't selling yet, 37% had no estate plan and 34% had not put structures in place to minimize taxes and shield the proceeds.

The professionals who work on these sales describe the same thing from their side. BNY Wealth's 2026 report on private business owners drew on 354 attorneys, investment bankers and CPAs. They recommend a two-year preparation runway and say most owners spend six to 18 months. Roughly three-quarters of them said pre-sale tax and wealth transfer planning can meaningfully increase net proceeds.

Neither report mentions announcements. My reading of the two together is that an announcement is a late signal. On the day a sale becomes public, the pre-sale planning is supposed to be finished already.

Owners who are looking to exit do turn to financial advisors

In the same UBS survey, 66% of owners who were looking to exit their businesses said they turned to financial advisors for advice. My judgment is that this is the stage where I'd want to be known. The survey doesn't say when that advisor came into the picture, whether it was someone new or someone the owner already had, and it doesn't say who ended up managing the proceeds.

A 2026 PNC Private Bank survey gives a sense of what owners want from an advisor. Of 300 owners of companies with $10 million to $125 million in revenue, 89% said they value financial advice that considers both business and personal needs, and 55% currently work with a financial advisor on both. Those are larger companies than many advisors' owner clients run, and the margin of error is plus or minus 8%. Even so, there is a wide gap between what those owners say they value and what they have.

What about a note after the sale?

The money has to go somewhere, and I wouldn't tell you a note sent after a sale can't work. Nothing I've found measures how often it does. The one figure I have on people who have been through an event is a small one, and it doesn't cover timing. Long Angle, a membership community of high-net-worth investors, published a poll in 2026 of 116 members who had been through a liquidity event. Of those, 47.4% said they manage everything themselves, 37.9% found their team through recommendations from peers, and 15.5% found it through existing financial professionals. The poll allowed more than one answer, the group is self-selected, and a peer community will lean toward peers, so I wouldn't stretch it to owners in general.

What I take from it is modest. The poll didn't measure notes from strangers. It does show that in that group a large share ran the money themselves or asked their friends, and that is what I'd expect a cold note to be up against. So I'd change what the note is for. If it asks for a meeting about the proceeds, it is one of however many the person received that month. If it comes from a peer with nothing attached, it can start a relationship that counts at the next decision.

There usually is a next decision. A sale is followed by taxes, reinvestment and estate planning, and an executive often has more shares still to vest. The first event is public. I think an advisor who is known by the time the later ones arrive has a better chance of being the one who is asked.

Use the event to choose who to know, and start before the next one

For business owners, that means choosing who to know well before any announcement. I'd pick owners whose companies and stage of life fit your practice, expect the first conversations to be about the business as much as the portfolio, and start as one professional to another, with no ask. The people most likely to know a sale is coming are the owner's CPA and attorney, and those relationships are worth having for the same reason.

For executives, the public filing is useful in a different way. It tells you someone's stage of life and roughly the size of what they're dealing with, which is most of what you need to judge whether they fit your practice. I'd keep the list short, hold it to your standards, and treat the first note as the start of a relationship.

You can test the idea on your own book. Of the last several clients who came to you with new liquidity, how many did you know before the money moved? Whatever the answer is, it tells you more about your market than a national survey can.

Common Questions

What counts as a liquidity event for a financial advisor?

A business sale, an IPO or stock sale, a large RSU vest, an inheritance, or a significant property sale. It's anything that turns a meaningful amount of wealth from illiquid to liquid and brings with it a decision about who manages it.

How far in advance should an advisor build this kind of relationship?

Nothing I've found measures when owners choose an advisor relative to a sale, so I can't give you a researched number. The attorneys, investment bankers and CPAs in BNY Wealth's 2026 report on private business owners recommend two years of preparation for the sale itself. An advisor who wants to be part of that preparation has to be known before it starts, so I'd think in years.
