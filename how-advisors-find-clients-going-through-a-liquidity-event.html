How Advisors Find Clients Going Through A Liquidity Event

The moment a sale closes or a stock vests, half the advisors in town call the same household. Here's how to already be the name they call, by getting there years earlier.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Liquidity events, a business sale, a stock vest, an inheritance, create new AUM fast, which is exactly why every advisor in the area is chasing the same household the moment it happens.
  • Reaching out right after the news breaks puts you in a crowd, competing with bankers, insurance agents, and the household's own advisor already circling.
  • The households tend to know an event is coming long before anyone else does. That window is the actual opportunity, not the week after the wire transfer clears.
  • A relationship built years ahead, peer to peer, with no ask attached, is the one that gets remembered when the money actually moves.
  • Assets in motion favor whoever the family already trusts, not whoever responds fastest once the liquidity hits.

Advisors learn this pattern the hard way. A founder sells the business, or a tech executive's shares finally vest, and within a week they've heard from three advisors, a banker, an insurance agent, and a wealth manager a cousin recommended. Everyone read the same public filing or heard the same news secondhand, and everyone is dialing at once. The household didn't ask for any of it, and they can feel the timing of every single call.

That crowd is the real problem with chasing liquidity events the way most of the industry teaches it. The event itself isn't a secret. By the time it's visible, there usually isn't time left to become the trusted one. That takes years, not a news cycle.

Why the moment of the event is the worst time to meet someone

Think about what the household is actually experiencing right as a sale closes or a large block of stock vests. They're fielding calls from people they've never spoken to before, all of whom suddenly want a meeting now that there's money to manage. It feels exactly like what it is: a crowd of people who showed up because there's suddenly money to manage. Wealthy families develop a fast filter for that, and an advisor who only appears once the liquidity is visible gets sorted into the same pile as everyone else who just read the news.

The advisors who win these households almost never introduced themselves at the moment of the event. They were already in the picture, often for years, long before there was anything to manage.

What assets in motion really means for an advisor's growth

Industry language calls this "assets in motion," the stretch where a household's money is about to move from illiquid to liquid, or from one advisor's shelf to someone else's. The useful insight is that most of these events have a long runway before they're public. A founder usually knows a sale is somewhere on the horizon for a year or more before it closes. An executive on an RSU vesting schedule knows the date. An inheritance often has warning signs well before a death.

That runway is the real opportunity, not the week after the wire clears. An advisor who's already a known, trusted peer during that stretch, with no pitch attached, is in an entirely different position than one who shows up the day the news breaks.

Common Questions

How do financial advisors find clients going through a liquidity event?

Mostly by already knowing them before the event happens. A business sale, a stock vest, or an inheritance draws several advisors at the same moment, so the one who already has a relationship, built peer to peer well in advance, almost always has the advantage over whoever shows up fastest once the news is public.

What counts as a liquidity event for a financial advisor?

A business sale, an IPO or stock sale, a large RSU vest, an inheritance, or a significant property sale. Anything that moves a meaningful amount of money from illiquid to liquid, and with it, a decision about who manages it.

Is it too late to reach a prospect after their liquidity event is already public?

Not too late, but much harder. Once a sale or vest is public, the household hears from every advisor, banker, and insurance agent who read the same news. Reaching out before the event, as a peer with no ask attached, is the version that actually gets remembered.

How far in advance should an advisor build this kind of relationship?

Years, when possible. Founders and executives often know a sale or a vest is somewhere on the horizon long before it happens. The advisor who was already a known, trusted peer during that stretch is the one the household calls first when the timing turns.
