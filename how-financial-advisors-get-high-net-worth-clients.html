How Financial Advisors Get High-Net-Worth Clients

In survey data, more than half of advised investors with $500,000 or more talked to one advisor before they hired. If that's how the choice gets made, the work that counts happens before anyone starts looking.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • More often than not, there is no contest to win. In a 2023 Dynasty Financial Partners survey of 1,000 investors with at least $500,000, 57% said the advisor they hired was the only one they had spoken to.
  • Referrals matter, and at the top they are about half the story. In a 2026 FiComm Partners survey, 49% of investors with $5 million or more said no referral was involved in finding their advisor.
  • A retiring advisor is a common reason clients move. Asked why they switched advisors, 30% in the Dynasty survey said their advisor retired.
  • So I'd work on being the advisor a family already knows. I'd choose those families on fit and timing, and I'd build the relationships on purpose alongside referrals.

I spent twenty years in this business before this firm existed. I ran thousands of seminars and worked with over 325 clients, most of them advisors and wealth managers, and the question I heard most was some version of this one: how do I get in front of families with real money?

The natural place to work on that is the meeting itself, on the assumption that a family interviews a few advisors and picks the best one. The survey research on how people with money choose an advisor describes something different. More often than not there was only one advisor in the conversation, so I'd put less effort into being impressive in the meeting and more into being known before it.

The investor surveys below asked people who already have an advisor, so they say nothing about people who never hired one. Dynasty runs a service that matches investors with advisors, which gives it a stake in the subject. Its floor was $500,000, and its release doesn't give the number of respondents who had switched advisors. The Cerulli and Kitces figures come from advisors of every kind and aren't specific to wealthy households, and the Kitces report dates from 2020. None of this research tests my own approach, and I'll say where I go past it.

More than half of these clients never compared advisors

In 2023, Dynasty Financial Partners and Absolute Engagement surveyed 1,000 investors who each had at least $500,000 investable and were already working with an advisor. One question asked how many advisors they had a conversation with before hiring their current one, and 57% said the advisor they hired was the only one they had spoken to.

For that majority there was never a meeting where several advisors competed. One advisor got the conversation and was hired. That moves the competition earlier. If a family is going to talk to one advisor, what matters is whose name they already have when they decide it's time, and that is settled before the first meeting, sometimes long before.

At the top, a referral was involved about half the time

The usual answer to how a family gets that name is a referral, and referrals deserve the credit they get. Cerulli Associates' U.S. Advisor Metrics 2025 report, released in February 2026, says referrals from clients, friends or family account for 54.2% of new clients for advisors.

The picture changes when you ask investors and sort them by wealth. A 2026 FiComm Partners and Absolute Engagement survey of 1,000 investors who work with an advisor found that among those with $5 million or more investable, 50% said a referral was involved in finding their advisor and 49% said no referral was involved. At $1 million to $4.9 million, 44% said no referral was involved, and at $500,000 to $999,000 the figure was 43%.

Referrals still matter in that data. What stands out to me is the 49%, because these are the wealthiest investors in the survey. The survey doesn't say how they found their advisor instead. It does say that for about half of this group a referral wasn't part of how they got there, so a practice that grows by referral alone is counting on the other half.

A retiring advisor is an opening you can see coming

Dynasty also asked people who had switched advisors why they did. Their advisor retiring was the reason for 30%, investment performance for 24%, and 18% said they didn't feel like a valued client.

The retirement figure is the one I'd think about. Advisors spend a lot of energy wondering who is unhappy with their current advisor, and in this survey retirement was named about as often as performance. People whose advisor is retiring aren't necessarily unhappy with anything. They are about to need someone, and I'd expect an advisor they already know a little to have a head start.

Why not rely on referrals alone?

On efficiency, nothing in the data beats them. A Kitces report on how financial planners market their services, published in 2020, measured first-year revenue for each dollar spent, and referrals returned 19X for the top 25% of advisors and 1.7X for the bottom 25%. That is a very wide range inside one channel. If the report ranked advisors by their results, some of that gap is built in, so I take the range as a fact and don't read a cause into it.

My reason for building something alongside referrals is control. A referral arrives when someone else thinks of you. It comes with trust and no promise of fit, and the friend of a great client may hold a fraction of what your practice is built for. I'd like an advisor to also have relationships they chose.

Choose the families on fit and timing, then build the group on purpose

Wealth alone tells you neither fit nor timing. Fit is whether the household is the size and complexity your practice is built for. Timing is whether something is coming: a sale, a retirement, or an advisor of their own who is close to retiring.

I'd build that group on purpose instead of waiting for it to form, which is the work I do with advisors. Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of high-net-worth individuals who actually want to meet and speak with you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the network and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

I keep it peer to peer, one professional getting to know another person, because that is how I've seen trust form with people who have money, and because it keeps your book selective. No study I know of tests that against the alternatives, so weigh it as my judgment. The research and the follow-up run every day through Jarvis, my agentic technology, so the relationships keep moving while you're with clients.

Two results from working this way

Chris, a VP at Merrill, landed $60 million in new AUM off a single conversation using this approach. Pete, a client of mine, landed a $100 million investor building the relationship the same way. In both cases the two people already knew each other by the time there was something to decide. Those are two results, and what happens for any one advisor depends on the advisor.

Common Questions

Do seminars still work for landing wealthy clients?

They can. I ran thousands of them. The best data I know of, a Kitces report on advisor marketing published in 2020, shows seminars returning 0.4X first-year revenue per dollar for the bottom 25% of advisors, which is less than they spent, at least in the first year. That data isn't specific to wealthy households. My read is that what a seminar returns has a lot to do with how well it's run.

How long does it take to land a high-net-worth client?

I haven't found reliable research on how long these relationships take to form, so this answer is my experience. One conversation can be enough when the relationship behind it is already in place. Getting to that point takes consistent work over weeks and months, and I'd be wary of anyone who promises it faster.

Do wealthy investors prefer large firms over independent advisors?

A good share of affluent investors do. The Cerulli Edge, U.S. Managed Accounts Edition for the first quarter of 2024, reported that among advised affluent investors, 39% prefer an advisor at a large, national organization and 18% prefer a locally owned practice. That figure covers affluent investors as a group, so I wouldn't read it as a finding about the very wealthy. If you run an independent practice, I wouldn't assume that being small is a selling point by itself. A family has to know you well enough that the size of the firm stops being the question.

If you're serious about growing your book with genuinely high-net-worth clients, grab time on my calendar. I read every one of these myself.
