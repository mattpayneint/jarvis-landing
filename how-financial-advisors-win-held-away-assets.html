How Do Financial Advisors Win Held-Away Assets From Their Best Clients?

The fastest AUM growth available to most advisors doesn't require a single new client. It's sitting in an account a current client already has somewhere else, and winning it has more to do with who else you know than with asking for it.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • The growth that's easiest to miss already belongs to a client you have. An old 401(k), a spouse's account, a chunk still sitting with a rival advisor. It's held away, not gone.
  • The clue is in how HNW relationships actually run. A March 2025 Journal of Financial Planning study found that HNW respondents were more likely than lower-wealth respondents to say their adviser coordinates with their CPA and other outside advisers.
  • Coordination shows up as a feature of good HNW relationships. The study doesn't say coordinating wins the money. It says the advisors already inside a client's full financial life look different from the ones managing one account in isolation.
  • So I'd work on becoming that coordinating point, peer to peer with the client's CPA, before I'd work on a script that asks for the rest of the wallet directly.

Most of the advisors I've worked with over twenty years, 325 and counting, already have the client. What they don't have is the whole client: the 401(k) from the job before this one, the account a spouse keeps at their own bank, the position still sitting with a prior advisor out of habit more than loyalty. None of that shows up as lost business, because it was never yours to lose. It's just sitting somewhere else, which is a very different problem than finding a new household.

Most of the growth conversation in this business is about acquisition, and I write about that elsewhere. This page is about the AUM that's already adjacent to a client you already have, and why I think the way to win it looks nothing like asking for it.

Held-away money just isn't coordinated

I think advisors underrate this because it doesn't feel like a growth channel. A new client is visible: a name, a first meeting, a signed agreement. A held-away account is invisible until someone mentions it, usually at tax time, usually to the CPA, not to you. The client isn't hiding it. It's just not part of the conversation you're having with them.

Nobody in the client's life is currently the person who sees the whole picture, so the account stays where it is by default, not because anyone decided to keep it there.

What the research on HNW coordination actually shows

Sommer and Lutter published a study in the Journal of Financial Planning in March 2025, built from a Dynata panel of 1,000 U.S. decision-makers with $250,000 or more, 383 of them with $1 million or more. Among the findings: HNW respondents were more likely than the lower-wealth group to say their adviser coordinates with CPAs and other outside advisers.

The study didn't put a number on how much more likely, and it says plainly that it didn't directly measure trust. So I can't tell you coordination moves a specific share of held-away money, because nobody has measured that. What the finding tells me is narrower and still useful: once a relationship reaches HNW territory, coordination with the client's other professionals becomes a more common feature of it. That's a description of what a good HNW relationship already looks like, closer to something you grow into than a script you run.

Why I wouldn't just ask for it

The obvious move is to ask directly: do you have accounts anywhere else you'd like me to look at. I've seen that question asked plenty of times, and it usually gets a polite non-answer, because it puts the client on the spot and frames the conversation around your AUM instead of their situation. A client doesn't owe you a full accounting of their finances just because you manage one piece of it.

What I've watched work instead is becoming the professional the client's other professionals already treat as a peer. When their CPA already talks to you like a colleague, not a stranger asking for referrals, the full picture tends to surface on its own, usually when the CPA is doing their own job and the advisor's name comes up as someone already in the loop.

What this looks like in practice

Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks around each client, between you and the CPAs and attorneys who already know their full financial picture. We're not making introductions for you. We create the relationship and the familiarity, then you take it from there directly, the same way you'd get to know any other professional in your world.

I keep this peer to peer and not transactional, because a CPA who feels like a referral source being worked will stop picking up the phone. The daily consistency behind it runs through Jarvis, my agentic technology, so those relationships keep moving while you're in client meetings.

Common Questions

What are held-away assets, exactly?

Any money a client has that you don't manage. An old 401(k) at a former employer, a spouse's account at a bank, a chunk still sitting with a rival advisor, a self-directed brokerage account nobody talks about at the review meeting. It's theirs, and it's not on your books.

Should I just ask clients directly about money they haven't moved over?

I wouldn't lead with that question, because it puts the client on the spot and makes the conversation about your AUM instead of their situation. I'd rather be the advisor who already talks to their CPA as a peer, so the full picture comes up naturally when the CPA is doing their job, not because you asked for it.

How much of a client's wallet share is normal to expect?

I haven't found research that gives a normal number, and I'd be skeptical of anyone who quotes one with confidence. What I've seen with my clients is that it tends to rise slowly, as the relationship earns more of the client's trust, not in a single conversation.

Does becoming a CPA's peer actually help with held-away assets?

I believe it does, from what I've watched happen with my own clients, but I don't have a study that measures it directly. The research I can point to shows that coordination with CPAs is more common among HNW relationships generally. It doesn't measure whether that coordination moves money.
