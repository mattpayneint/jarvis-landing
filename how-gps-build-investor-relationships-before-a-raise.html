How Do GPs Build Investor Relationships Before a Raise Even Opens?

Most GPs start the clock on a raise the day the data room opens. The clock that actually decides it starts earlier than that, and the data on how long raises now take makes the gap between the two expensive.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • LPs say they find new managers mostly through their own networks. A 2026 iConnections Global Allocator Report found nearly 80% of LPs report discovering new managers through professional networks, with a much smaller share coming through databases or cold outreach.
  • Raises now take longer than they used to. Preqin's 2025 data shows the average time to close a fund across private capital rose from 16 months in 2020 to 25 months by July 2025.
  • LPs have less room to commit than they did, too. Bain's Global Private Equity Report 2026 found 53% of LPs are limited in making new commitments because past commitments are still undrawn.
  • So I'd treat relationship building as work that runs ahead of the raise, not as work that starts the day the raise does.

Most of the fund managers I talk with measure the length of a raise from the day they start calling it a raise, the first pitch meeting, the first time the deck goes out. The data on how LPs actually say they find managers, and on how long the whole process now takes, points to an earlier start line.

Everything here is private capital and private equity data, mostly institutional, not a study built on syndicators or family offices specifically. I'll be straight about where it stops and where my own judgment, from working this with clients, picks up.

Where LPs say new managers actually come from

The 2026 iConnections Global Allocator Report asked institutional investors how they discover new managers. Nearly 80% said professional networks, and over half named conferences and industry events as a primary channel. The report describes databases and cold outreach as a much smaller share. The survey allows more than one answer, so these figures aren't shares of one pie, and iConnections sells conferences and introductions to allocators, which gives it a stake in the answer.

Read plainly, that says the network has to already exist before a manager is found through it. A professional network isn't something you assemble in the three weeks before a first close. It's either already there when the raise opens or it isn't, and if it isn't, you're starting the formal raise and the relationship building at the same time, instead of having done one before the other.

The raise itself now runs longer

Preqin's 2025 data shows the average time to close a fund across private capital rose from 16 months in 2020 to 25 months by July 2025, and the total number of funds in market has grown by more than 240% over the same stretch. More managers are asking, and each ask takes longer to land. That figure is private capital broadly, not a number for any one strategy or manager size, so I wouldn't apply it to a specific syndication or deal-by-deal raise. What it tells me is which way things are moving, and that it's slower, with more competition for the same LP attention.

LPs also have less room than they did

Bain's Global Private Equity Report 2026 found 53% of LPs are limited in making new commitments because past commitments are still undrawn, up 15 percentage points from year-end 2024. Distributions as a share of net asset value were flat at 14%, a level last seen in 2008 to 2009. That's a private equity figure and doesn't speak to family offices or individual investors directly, but it matches a pattern I see with clients. An investor who's genuinely within mandate for your fund can still have nothing to put to work this quarter. That's a timing problem, not a verdict on the fund.

Two clocks, and most GPs only start one of them

Line up the network data against the timing data and there are really two clocks running on any raise. One is the raise clock, the 16 to 25 months from first pitch to final close. The other is the relationship clock, the time it takes a GP and an investor to actually get to know each other well enough that a commitment is a reasonable next step. I don't have a study that measures the second clock. What I do know from watching raises is that it doesn't start when the first clock does, and it can't be compressed by moving faster through the pitch.

The raises I've seen go smoothly don't wait on introductions. They start the relationship first, peer to peer, with allocators who were already within mandate and aligned with the thesis, long before anyone's asked to invest. By the time the formal ask happens, the relationship clock has already run most of its course, and the raise clock is the only one still ticking.

Common Questions

Doesn't a cold introduction still work if the fund is strong enough?

I can't give you a conversion rate for cold outreach versus an existing relationship, because no study I've found measures that comparison directly. What the 2026 iConnections Global Allocator Report found is that nearly 80% of LPs say they discover new managers through their own professional networks, with a much smaller share coming through databases or cold outreach. iConnections sells conferences and introductions to allocators, so keep that in mind. It's a self-report of where discovery happens, not a measurement of what actually closes a commitment.

How long should I expect a raise to take once I officially start it?

Preqin's 2025 data puts the average time to close a fund across all of private capital at 25 months in July 2025, up from 16 months in 2020. That figure covers private capital broadly, not syndications or deal-by-deal raises specifically, and the page doesn't explain how the average was calculated. I'd treat it as a sign that the window has gotten longer industry-wide, not a promise about your own timeline.

Is it legal to build these relationships before I officially open my raise?

That depends on your offering exemption and is a question for your securities counsel, not me. If you're raising under Rule 506(b), there are real limits on general solicitation before and during a raise. I'm not a placement agent, I'm not licensed to raise or place capital, and I don't make investor introductions. What I work on with a GP is the relationship building that happens around that legal boundary, not inside it.

What if an investor I already know isn't allocating right now?

Bain's Global Private Equity Report 2026 found 53% of LPs say they're limited in making new commitments because past commitments are still undrawn, up 15 percentage points from year-end 2024. That's a private equity figure, not a measurement of family offices or individual investors specifically, but it's consistent with what I see. A within-mandate investor who isn't committing today isn't a no. I'd keep that relationship warm with real news, not a check-in asking if anything has changed.
