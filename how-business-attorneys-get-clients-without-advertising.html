How Business Attorneys Get More Clients Without Advertising

Most owners start preparing for a sale later than the people who advise them recommend. For a business attorney, I think that makes the owner who is two or three years from a deal, and hasn't called anyone yet, the one worth knowing now.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Owners prepare for a sale late. In a 2023 UBS survey of more than 500 U.S. business owners, 70% of those who had sold in recent years said they spent less than two years preparing for the exit, and eight in 10 wished they had started earlier.
  • So the owner worth knowing is the one who hasn't started. My judgment is that counsel hired in the middle of a deal is chosen in a hurry, from the names the owner already has.
  • That is a relationship you can begin without advertising, as a peer, years before there is a matter. On advertising itself, I have no evidence either way for business attorneys.

Picture an owner who receives a letter of intent on a Tuesday. He needs counsel this week, and if he is like most sellers in the research I've read, he started getting ready later than he would have liked. In a 2023 UBS survey of more than 500 U.S. business owners, each with at least one employee and $1 million in annual revenue, 70% of the owners who had sold in recent years said they spent less than two years preparing for the exit. Eight in 10 wished they had started preparations earlier.

The professionals who advise sellers describe the same thing from their side. For its 2026 Insights for Private Business Owners report, BNY Wealth surveyed 354 of them: attorneys, investment bankers and CPAs. They recommend a two-year preparation runway and report that most owners spend six to 18 months. By their account, only 48% of sellers are prepared when buyer due diligence begins. And 80% say building a cohesive deal team is critical, with the strongest teams assembled early.

What those two surveys cover

Both are about preparation. Neither asked how an owner chooses a business attorney or when he hires one, and I have not found a study that does. UBS and BNY both sell services to owners like these, the UBS sample was defined by company revenue and not personal wealth, and the BNY figures are professionals describing their own clients. So the research carries one point, that preparation for a sale tends to start late. What that means for a business attorney is my professional judgment.

A late start means counsel gets chosen in a hurry

Go back to the owner with the letter of intent. In my experience, his first calls go to people he already knows: his CPA, his banker, another owner who has sold a company. A stranger has to be found, checked out and trusted inside a few days. Someone the owner has known for two years has already been checked out.

The two-year figure is what I'd build on. If the people who do this work want two years and most owners give it less, then many of the owners you would most like to represent in a sale are running their companies today and not looking for a lawyer. As far as they know, there is nothing to look for yet. That is when I'd want them to know you, because it is also when knowing a business attorney can do them the most good: there is still time to get ready.

Growing without advertising is a preference

I have no evidence either way on advertising for business attorneys, so I won't tell you it fails. Growing without it is a preference, mine and that of most of the attorneys I work with. For a practice where a handful of the right matters a year carries the firm, I would rather spend the effort on being well known to the owners and CPAs most likely to have one of those matters than on reaching a broad audience and sorting through it afterward. If your work is high in volume and the client has nobody to ask, being found quickly may matter more.

What I'd do: know the owners who are a few years out

I'd get known, as a peer, by owners who are a few years from a sale and by the CPAs and bankers around them. That takes consistency over months, which is hard to keep up while you are running deals. It's the part I handle.

Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of business owners and the CPAs and bankers around them who actually want to meet and speak with you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the network and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

The daily consistency runs through Jarvis, my agentic technology, while you do the legal work only you can do.

Neil, a business attorney who works with founders, wrote $75,000 in new business within his first two weeks working with me, all of it from CPAs and founders getting to know him as a peer before any pitch happened. One attorney's result is no forecast of yours, and two weeks is faster than I would tell anyone to expect.

Which owners are in that position for your practice, and how you'd open the first conversation, is what I'd want to work out with you.
