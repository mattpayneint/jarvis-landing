How Business Attorneys Get More Clients Without Advertising

Founders and business owners don't search for counsel. They ask the CPA, the banker, or the founder down the hall who they already trust. Here's how business attorneys get in that circle on purpose.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Business attorneys get real matters through relationships, with CPAs, bankers, other attorneys, and past clients, almost never through advertising.
  • Advertising mostly reaches commodity work. The complex, high-value matters move through trust, the same way a founder picks a banker.
  • Bar advertising and solicitation rules already push live, pressure-based marketing toward real risk, so the safest path and the most effective one point the same direction.
  • Referral partners are genuinely valuable, but they decide the pace. A practice that only grows by referral grows at the speed of somebody else's memory.
  • The alternative is building direct relationships with the CPAs, bankers, and founders in your world on purpose, peer to peer, before there's a deal to send your way.

Picture a founder two weeks from closing a raise, or a business owner quietly shopping the company. Nobody in that position opens a search engine and types "business attorney near me." They call their CPA, or their banker, or a founder friend who just went through the same thing, and they ask who handled it well. The name that comes back is the one that gets the call.

That single fact explains why law firm advertising mostly produces the matters nobody wants: small contract reviews, price-sensitive entity formations, the occasional tire kicker. The attorneys with the best corporate and transactional books tend to look like they do no marketing at all, because they're positioned inside the rooms where the actual decision gets made, usually after years of just being a good lawyer and a decent person at the right dinners.

The question worth asking is whether you can get into those rooms on purpose, faster than twenty years of accidental networking. I've spent two decades working with professionals who sell trust for a living, attorneys among them, and the answer is yes.

Why advertising undersells business attorneys

Founders and owners are pitched constantly, by vendors, by lenders, by every service provider orbiting a growing company. They've built the same filter toward legal marketing that they have toward everything else: whoever is visibly selling gets screened out, whoever is trusted gets the call. An ad, no matter how well written, puts a business attorney in the first category with the exact clients worth having.

There's a practical angle too. The matters that matter, a raise, an acquisition, a messy partnership dispute, don't start with a Google search. They start with a phone call to someone the founder already respects. Advertising can fill a volume practice built on contract templates. It rarely builds the kind of practice that sits across the table from real deals.

The bar rules already agree with you

Most states restrict live, pressure-based solicitation of a specific prospect more than they restrict general advertising or peer relationship building, and the details vary by state. Practically, that means the version of growth that already fits how business owners choose counsel, relationship first, is also the version that keeps you furthest from the lines your bar cares about.

CPAs and bankers see the deal first

Referral relationships with CPAs, bankers, and other attorneys are worth building, and nothing here argues otherwise. But look at the structure honestly. The CPA sends you a client when they remember to, when their usual attorney is slammed, at a pace with nothing to do with your capacity or your goals. You're one name in a chain you don't control, and most of the people ahead of you in it already have their own favorite attorney.

A practice that grows only through that chain grows in lurches. Good quarters and quiet quarters show up at random, and neither has much to do with the quality of your work.

Becoming the attorney a founder already trusts

The alternative is being in the room before the deal exists. Business law has its own rhythm: quiet stretches, then a raise, a sale, a partnership blowup, and suddenly the founder needs serious counsel this week. When that moment lands, they don't run a search. They call the attorney they already know.

So the whole game is becoming that attorney, for more founders, on purpose. Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of business owners and the CPAs and bankers around them who actually want to meet and speak with you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the network and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

The approach stays peer to peer the entire time, so nothing about it reads as solicitation to the founder, to the CPA watching, or to your own bar. The daily consistency, the research, the relationship building, runs through Jarvis, my agentic operator, while you do the legal work only you can do.

That's how business attorneys actually get more clients: being known by the right people before the deal is sitting on the table.

Common Questions

How do business attorneys get clients without advertising?

Through the CPAs, bankers, and other founders already in their world. Founders and business owners pick counsel the same way they pick an accountant, by asking someone they trust. Attorneys who grow their corporate and transactional work build direct relationships inside those circles deliberately, peer to peer, instead of waiting to be recommended.

Do bar advertising rules make marketing risky for business attorneys?

State rules on lawyer advertising and solicitation vary, and live, pressure-based contact aimed at one specific prospect is the version attorneys should be most careful with. Relationship building among peers carries far less of that risk, and it happens to be the version that actually produces business-owner clients.

Are referrals from CPAs and bankers enough to grow a business law practice?

They're valuable, and nothing here replaces them. The issue is control. A CPA sends a deal when they remember to and when their favorite attorney is busy. A practice that only grows by referral grows at the pace of somebody else's memory, not your own.

What's the fastest way for a business attorney to find new clients?

Build direct relationships with the CPAs, bankers, and founders you'd want sending you work, before there's a deal to send. The first real matter usually follows a relationship that already existed, not a pitch that just arrived.
