Why Investors Ghost Fund Managers

Silence after a good first call usually gets read as a lost sale. In my experience it's more often a problem of fit, timing or authority, and you can learn two of those before the meeting.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • In my experience, silence after a good meeting is rarely about interest. It's usually hiding one of three other things: the fund is outside their mandate, they aren't committing new capital right now, or the person you met can't move it forward.
  • One number is consistent with the timing part. Bain's Global Private Equity Report 2026 says 53% of LPs are limited in making new commitments because past commitments are still undrawn, up 15 percentage points from year-end 2024.
  • Sometimes the silence is a verdict. LPs in Goldman Sachs Asset Management's 2025 survey prioritize fees and terms, track records, and team stability. A follow-up doesn't fix a miss on those.
  • The useful move is to learn fit and timing before the meeting, so that a quiet inbox afterward tells you something.

The first call went well. They asked real questions and said to send the deck, and maybe they mentioned a number. You sent it that afternoon. Three weeks and two polite follow-ups later, nothing has come back.

Most managers I talk with take that personally, and then they start reworking the deck. I'd look somewhere else first. My experience is that in a lot of these cases the investor liked the fund fine and had no room to act on it, and nobody on their side had any reason to tell you so.

What the silence may be hiding How you'd know What I'd do
Fit. The fund is outside their investment mandate. What they've backed before sits in other asset classes, stages or check sizes. Let it go kindly. Liking you doesn't change what their mandate covers.
Timing. Within mandate, but not committing right now. They're at target allocation, waiting on distributions, or have paused new managers. Keep the relationship. Send a real update when something changes.
Authority. The person you met can't advance it. They were enthusiastic, and the decision gets made somewhere else. Ask how a decision like this gets made there, and who is part of it.
Interest. They looked and passed. Fit, timing and authority were all in place, and it still went quiet. Take it as a no. One update with real news, then move on.

LPs have less room to commit than they did, and the numbers show it

Bain's Global Private Equity Report 2026 says 53% of LPs are limited in making new commitments because their past commitments are still undrawn. That's up 15 percentage points from year-end 2024. The same report puts distributions at 14% of net asset value, a level last seen in 2008 to 2009, and says they've held below 15% for four years running. Money that would normally have come back to LPs and been recommitted is sitting in companies that haven't sold. Bain counts almost 40% of portfolio companies as held more than five years, up from 29% in 2019.

At the same time, far more managers are asking. Preqin's 2025 data shows total funds in market have increased by over 240% since 2020, and the average time to close a fund across private capital rose from 16 months in 2020 to 25 months in July 2025.

That data is private equity and private capital broadly, and Bain's summary doesn't say which LPs it surveyed. None of it measures investors who go quiet after a meeting, and I haven't seen a study of how family offices or individual investors behave after one. What the numbers do show is the environment every manager is raising in: LPs with less room, more funds in line, and a longer road to a close. In that environment a person can enjoy the call, mean every word of it, and still have nothing to commit.

A good first meeting proves interest and nothing else

This is the framework I use with clients. It's mine, built from watching raises, and I'm not presenting it as a research finding. Four things have to be true before a conversation can turn into a commitment, and I check them in this order: fit, timing, authority, interest.

Fit means the fund sits within the investor's mandate: asset class, stage, check size. Timing means they're putting new money to work now. Authority means the person you met can move a decision forward. Interest comes last, because it's the only one of the four you can feel on a call, and it's the one that tells you the least.

When a good meeting goes quiet afterward, I look for which of the first three was missing. The CIO who loved the thesis may be at target allocation for the year, or the partner who asked the sharp questions may not cover private funds at all. Neither of them owes you that explanation, and writing an email to say "we like you and we're full" is work nobody volunteers for. So you get silence, and silence looks the same whichever reason is behind it.

Sometimes the silence is a verdict, and a follow-up won't change it

The strongest objection to everything above is that I'm letting managers off the hook. Sometimes the investor looked hard and passed. In Goldman Sachs Asset Management's 2025 Private Markets Diagnostic Survey, LPs said they prioritize fees and terms, track records, and team stability, and the report describes them making fewer but larger commitments with managers they already have. One older academic study points the same way. Kaplan and Schoar's 2005 paper in the Journal of Finance found that better-performing partnerships are more likely to raise follow-on funds. Both sources are institutional, and the study is old, but I've seen nothing that contradicts them.

So if the record or the terms didn't clear the bar, I don't think any sequence of emails will rescue it. What you can do is find out which kind of silence you're looking at, and that takes knowing the investor's mandate and pacing.

Learn fit and timing before the meeting

What I'd change is the order of operations. Most managers I work with learn an investor's mandate and pacing after the pitch, if they ever learn it. I'd want to know both before the fund comes up at all, and the way to learn them is to have the relationship first, peer to peer. Two people in the same world can ask each other what they're allocating toward this year and whether they're adding managers. Between peers that's an ordinary question. From someone in the middle of a pitch it's an awkward one.

When you know those answers going in, silence becomes readable. An investor who's within mandate and paused until next year is a relationship to keep warm with real news: a first close, an anchor commitment, something that changed. An investor who had fit, timing and authority and still went quiet has given you an answer, and you can stop spending calendar on it.

For the ones who've already gone quiet on you, I'd send one matter-of-fact update when you have real news, and I wouldn't ask whether they've had a chance to look at the deck. That question hands them an easy "not yet," and you'll wait another month on it.

Common Questions

How many follow-ups are too many?

I stop at one update with real news after true silence. That's my practice, and I can't point to a study on it. My reasoning is that a second and third bump give the investor no new information, and they cost you some of the scarcity your raise depends on. If the reason for the silence is timing, a fourth email won't change their pacing.

How do fund managers get investors to respond?

I don't know a way to make anyone respond, and I wouldn't promise one. What I've seen help is knowing more before the meeting: whether the fund is within the investor's mandate, whether they're committing new capital now, and whether the person you're meeting can move a decision. Those are easier to learn inside a peer-to-peer relationship that started before the raise than in the middle of a pitch.
