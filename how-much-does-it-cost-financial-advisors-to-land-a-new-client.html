How Much Does It Really Cost a Financial Advisor to Land a New Client?

The channel most advisors treat as free turned out to be the most expensive one measured, once advisor time got counted. Here's what the data actually shows, and what I'd do differently.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • The average cost to land a new client is $3,119, and most of it isn't cash. A 2020 Kitces report on how financial planners market their services found $2,600 of that average was advisor time, not hard dollars.
  • The channel advisors treat as free is the most expensive one measured. Centers of influence ran $9,144 per client once time was counted, well ahead of paid advertising at $3,805.
  • Execution beats channel. Top-quartile advisors got 19 times their money back on referrals in the first year. Bottom-quartile advisors got 1.7 times on the same channel.
  • So I'd judge a channel by whether a deliberate process can make it efficient, not by its price tag.

Ask most advisors what it costs to land a new client and they'll talk about marketing spend: the ad budget, the event bill, maybe a CRM line item. Almost nobody counts their own hours. A 2020 Kitces report on how financial planners market their services put a number on the whole picture, hard dollars and time together.

The report's sample is given two different ways on the same page, more than 800 advisors in one spot and nearly 1,000 in another, and the data predates 2022, so I'd treat the exact dollar figures as dated rather than current. The pattern underneath them, that execution explains more of the gap than channel does, is the part I still rely on.

Where the real cost of a new client hides

Across the advisors Kitces surveyed, the average cost to land one new client was $3,119: $519 in hard dollars and $2,600 in advisor time. Broken out by channel, paid advertising ran $3,805 per client, networking $4,494, and centers of influence $9,144, the highest of any channel the report measured.

COI relationships don't show up on an invoice, which is probably why advisors rarely think of them as expensive. They cost hours instead, and hours are the one resource most advisors already have too little of. A lunch that doesn't lead anywhere costs the same two hours as one that does. The report just put a number on what that actually adds up to.

Execution explains the return far more than the channel does

The report also split results by how advisors ranked overall, top 25% against bottom 25%, and measured first-year revenue for every dollar spent. On referrals, the top quartile returned 19 times their money in the first year. The bottom quartile returned 1.7 times, barely clearing even, on that exact same channel.

The part I didn't expect is what happened lower on the list. Paid web listings returned 8.3 times for the top quartile, just ahead of COI relationships at 7.8 times, and marketing lists returned 7.2 times, close behind COI and still well ahead of what the bottom quartile got from any channel. Those two, paid listings and purchased lists, also scored poorly on advisor satisfaction with the prospects they produced, which is probably why the industry talks about them as channels that don't work for serious clients. The return data in this report says otherwise. What dragged on satisfaction was how those prospects felt to work, not what they were worth.

What I'd actually change about how an advisor grows AUM

I don't read this as a case for any one channel so much as a case against treating business development as hours scattered wherever the week leaves room. If a referral conversation or a COI lunch costs real money once you count your own time at it, it deserves the same discipline you'd put behind a paid campaign: who it's with, what it's for, and whether it's actually moving toward a household that fits your book.

Common Questions

Is a referral really free if it doesn't cost any money?

Not based on this data. The 2020 Kitces report found $2,600 of the $3,119 average cost per client was advisor time, not cash. A channel with no invoice can still be the most expensive thing you do that week.

Should advisors avoid purchased marketing lists or paid web listings?

Not based on this data. The same report found marketing lists returned 7.2 times first-year revenue and paid web listings returned 8.3 times for top-quartile advisors. What dragged those two channels down was advisor satisfaction with the prospects they produced, not the return.

Does spending more money bring in more AUM?

Not by itself. Execution explains most of the gap in this data. Top-quartile advisors turned referrals into 19 times first-year revenue while bottom-quartile advisors got 1.7 times on the same channel.

Is this data specific to high-net-worth households?

No. The Kitces sample wasn't limited to wealthy households and the data predates 2022. I'd treat it as a lens on advisor economics generally, not as a given for high-net-worth clients specifically.
