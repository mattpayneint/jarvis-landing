How Do GPs Qualify LPs Before The First Call?

In my experience, most GPs qualify an LP on two things: there's capital, and someone made the introduction. I qualify on four, in a set order, with interest last, and the one I see skipped most is timing.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • I qualify in this order: fit, timing, authority, interest. That's my framework, from working with fund managers. Interest goes last because it's the easiest to feel on a call and tells you the least.
  • Timing is the screen I see skipped most. Bain's Global Private Equity Report 2026 says 53% of LPs are limited in making new commitments because past commitments are still undrawn.
  • Capital and a warm introduction give you access. They leave the investor's mandate, pacing and decision-maker unknown.
  • Qualifying doesn't improve the fund. LPs in Goldman Sachs Asset Management's 2025 Private Markets Diagnostic Survey prioritize fees and terms, track records, and team stability. What qualifying changes is where your hours go.

Say a friend introduces you to an investor whose mandate covers exactly what your fund does. The call is warm, the questions are good, and twenty minutes in you hear that they finished their allocations for the year and you should come back next spring.

The introduction was sincere and the investor was gracious. But you can often learn that answer before the fund ever comes up, and the question behind it is the one I see managers skip: is this investor committing new capital to anyone this year?

A wasted conversation costs more than it used to

Preqin's data shows the average time to close a fund across private capital went from 16 months in 2020 to 25 months in July 2025, with total funds in market up over 240% since 2020. Bain's Global Private Equity Report 2026 adds that even in normal periods, roughly 15% of fund series fail to raise a successor fund.

Those are broad private capital and private equity figures, and so is Bain's figure on LP capacity that I use below. None is specific to a strategy or to emerging managers, and Bain's summary doesn't say which LPs it surveyed, so I can't tell you family offices or individuals are in the same position.

What they tell me is that a manager's hours with investors are the scarce resource in a raise. There are more funds asking, the road to a close is longer, and a close isn't guaranteed at the end of it. Under those conditions, a meeting that was never going to produce a commitment costs an hour of a partner's time, then the follow-up, then the weeks of hoping.

The order I qualify in: fit, timing, authority, interest

This is the framework I use with clients. It comes from my own work with fund managers, and nobody has tested it in a study, so take it as experience.

Fit is first. Does the fund sit within this investor's mandate on asset class, stage and check size? An office whose mandate covers buyout and private credit is not a fit for an early-stage venture fund, however much it likes the team.

Timing is second, and it's the one I see skipped most. Could they commit now? Bain's 2026 report says 53% of LPs are limited in making new commitments because past commitments are still undrawn, up 15 percentage points from year-end 2024. An investor who is at target allocation, or who has paused new managers until distributions come back, can be a relationship worth years of your attention and still be the wrong use of this quarter.

Authority is third. A title tells you less than you'd think. What matters is whether this person is responsible for evaluating and moving a commitment in your asset class.

Interest is last. It has to show up in what they do, and I don't try to talk anyone into it. I put it last because it's the easiest of the four to feel on a call, and it tells you the least when the other three are unknown. Plenty of warm meetings involve real interest from someone who has no mandate, no room, or no say.

A warm introduction gives you access

Introductions are wonderful. I'm not trying to replace them. An introduction gives you access, but it doesn't necessarily give you alignment. I've seen that in my own business. Good clients have referred people to me where the timing was off or it wasn't the right fit. The trust was real, and trust and fit turned out to be separate things.

The same goes for wealth. Net worth or AUM tells you they have capital. It tells you almost nothing about whether that capital is relevant to this fund at this time.

What qualifying can't do

Qualifying has two limits. The first is that you're working from the outside. Before a first call, an investor's mandate and pacing are a hypothesis, built from what they've backed before and what they say publicly. Sometimes the hypothesis is wrong. Unknown is an acceptable answer, as long as you treat it as unknown.

The second is that qualifying doesn't make the fund better. Goldman Sachs Asset Management's 2025 Private Markets Diagnostic Survey found LPs prioritize fees and terms, track records, and team stability, and Kaplan and Schoar's 2005 study in the Journal of Finance found that better-performing partnerships are more likely to raise follow-on funds. Both are institutional sources. A perfectly qualified investor will still pass on a fund that doesn't clear those. All qualifying does is make sure your best hours go to the investors who could say yes.

Do the qualifying before there's an ask

The practical question is how you learn any of this before a call. My answer is that you learn it in the relationship, and you start the relationship earlier than most managers I've worked with do. Before I'd reach for the calendar, I'd write down my best guess at each investor's side of the table, and what evidence I have for it. Then I'd let the first conversations be about them: what they're allocating toward, how they're pacing this year, how decisions get made there. Between peers, those are normal things to talk about. They're hard to ask once you've sent a deck.

Done that way, the first call about the fund comes at the end of the qualifying instead of the start of it. And the investors whose answer is "not this year" stay in your world for the next fund.

Todd, a friend running a fund, landed $35 million for his bourbon fund this way. Pete, a client of mine, landed a $100 million investor the same way. I can't promise anyone a raise like either of those.

Common Questions

What does within mandate mean?

It means the fund's asset class, stage, check size and strategy fit what that allocator's own investment mandate allows them to put money into. A family office can love your story, have plenty of capital, and still be unable to invest because your fund isn't something their mandate covers. Being within mandate only makes an investor eligible. They can be within mandate and fully committed for the year.

How many qualified LP conversations does a GP need?

I don't have a number, and I haven't found a study that gives one. It depends on the fund size and the check sizes you're targeting. My view is that the goal is a concentration of credible capital relationships, with allocators who are within mandate, deploying now and interested, more than a long list of meetings. With the average close across private capital at 25 months in Preqin's July 2025 data, I'd spend the hours where a commitment is possible.

If your calendar has more polite meetings than qualified ones, grab time on my calendar. I read every one of these myself.
