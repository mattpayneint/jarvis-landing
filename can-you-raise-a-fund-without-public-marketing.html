Can You Raise a Fund Without Public Marketing?

The pressure every GP feels eventually is to get more visible. The SEC's own numbers on where the money actually moved in 2025 point somewhere quieter.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Most GPs assume that at some point, raising enough capital means getting more publicly visible. The SEC's own 2025 data on Regulation D offerings doesn't back that up.
  • Of the Reg D dollars reported in 2025, $2,248.4 billion moved under Rule 506(b), which bars general solicitation, against $142.6 billion under Rule 506(c), which allows it. That's roughly 94% of the reported total moving through the exemption built around investors a GP already has a real relationship with.
  • The SEC itself says these statistics could underestimate true activity, and the figures don't show how any single GP actually built the relationships behind a commitment, only which legal path the offering was reported under.
  • So I'd build real, peer-to-peer relationships with people likely within your mandate well before the raise, rather than treating broader visibility as the fix.

I hear a version of the same worry from almost every manager I talk with: if the existing network isn't producing enough conversations, doesn't the answer eventually have to be getting the fund more visible, a bigger platform, more reach, something closer to public marketing. It's a reasonable worry. It's also not what the data on where capital actually moves supports.

Most of the reported money still moves under the quiet rule

The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's statistics on Regulation D offerings for 2025 show $2,248.4 billion raised under Rule 506(b), the exemption that does not permit general solicitation, against $142.6 billion under Rule 506(c), which does. Pooled investment funds, the category most GP vehicles fall into, reported $2,118.3 billion across 17,593 offerings with a median size of $3.6 million. However you slice it, the overwhelming share of reported Reg D dollars in 2025 moved through the path built around people an issuer already has a real relationship with, not the path built for reaching strangers.

I think that's worth sitting with, because it isn't my opinion about what works. It's the SEC's own count of where the money actually went.

What this data does and doesn't show

The SEC states plainly that these statistics could underestimate the true number of offerings and the true amount of capital raised, and that a single offering can claim more than one exemption, which can cause the counts to overlap. The figures are aggregate dollars and offering counts for a full year, not a measurement of how any one manager actually built the relationships that led to a commitment. The gap between the mean and the median in this data is also large, which means a relatively small number of very big funds can carry a lot of the total. None of that changes the basic split, but it's why I'd read this as a picture of the market's center of gravity, not a precise forecast for any one raise.

The quiet exemption and the relationship-first habit are the same thing

The part I think gets missed is this: Rule 506(b) isn't quiet by accident. It's built around the idea that the investor and the issuer already know each other well enough that a public pitch was never necessary in the first place. General solicitation is the tool you reach for when that prior relationship doesn't exist yet. So the legal structure most Reg D dollars move through, and the relationship-first way I'd build a raise anyway, aren't two separate things that happen to line up. They're the same fact seen twice: by the time real money moves, a real relationship was usually already there.

That reframes the original worry. If the existing network isn't producing enough conversations, the data doesn't say the fix is more reach. It says the fix is more relationships of the kind that make reach unnecessary.

The objection worth taking seriously

The strongest pushback here is that 506(c) exists for a reason, and $142.6 billion is still a very large number on its own. Some funds genuinely need to reach investors they have no prior connection to, and general solicitation is a legitimate way to do it. I'm not arguing it never works. I'm arguing it's the smaller share of where the reported money actually went in 2025, which means betting your whole strategy on becoming more publicly visible is a bet against where most of the capital in this market is already proven to move.

What I'd build instead

I'd treat the relationships themselves as the thing to build deliberately, well before a raise opens, with people likely within your mandate rather than the broadest possible audience. That's slower than a marketing push, and it's also the pattern the SEC's own numbers describe as already dominant. It keeps your raise's scarcity intact too, since a relationship that existed before you needed anything reads very differently than one that started with an ask.

Common Questions

Does this mean Rule 506(c) and general solicitation are a mistake?

No, and I'm not qualified to tell you which exemption fits your raise. That's a question for your securities counsel, based on your structure and your investor base. What I'm pointing at is simpler: the data shows most reported Reg D dollars in 2025 moved through the exemption that doesn't allow general solicitation, which tells me the market's center of gravity is still relationship-based, whatever exemption any one fund ultimately uses.

Is relationship-based fundraising actually required by law, or just common practice?

I'll leave the legal mechanics to counsel. What I can say in plain terms is that one path to a private offering is built around investors you already have a real, pre-existing relationship with, and the other is built around reaching people you don't. The SEC data shows the first path carrying far more of the reported dollars in 2025.

Does this apply to a $5 million emerging fund the same way it applies to a $500 million fund?

The SEC's own 2025 figures show a median 506(b) offering size of $2.6 million, well below the very large fund totals that can dominate an aggregate dollar figure. I read that as a sign that this isn't only how the largest managers raise. It's the median-sized offering too.

Should a GP ever use public marketing at all?

I'm not against visibility. Being known, through content or a public presence, can make a first real conversation easier. What I'd push back on is treating broader visibility as the fundraising strategy itself. The data suggests the commitment still tends to follow a relationship that existed before the pitch, not the reach of whatever got published.

If you'd rather build the relationships than chase the reach, grab time on my calendar. I read every one of these myself.
