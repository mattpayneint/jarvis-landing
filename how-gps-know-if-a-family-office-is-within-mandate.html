How Do GPs Know If a Family Office Is Actually Within Mandate?

A family office looks like the ideal LP on paper: one decision-maker, flexible capital, a taste for direct deals. The data on where family offices actually stand right now is more complicated than that, and so is who's making the call.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • "It's a family office" describes the wrapper, not the mandate. The fit question is the same one you'd ask an institution: asset class, stage, check size, thesis.
  • Two 2025 surveys show the asset class holding roughly steady. RBC and Campden Wealth found private markets at 29% of the average North American family office portfolio, down only slightly from 30% in 2024, with 88% holding some exposure. UBS found that offices planning changes intended to trim private markets from 21% to 18% on average.
  • The bigger shift is who's deciding. RBC and Campden Wealth found 69% of North American family offices had a succession plan in 2025, up from 53% the year before.
  • So I'd still run the full mandate check, fit, timing, authority, interest, and I'd ask who else is involved in the decision before I counted on one relationship holding.

Family offices have a reputation in this business as the easy allocator, a single principal who can write a check without an investment committee memo. A warm introduction to the right family office feels like it should be worth more than the same introduction into a large institution's manager-screening process.

I think that reputation gets the mandate question backwards. A family office being flexible doesn't mean it's flexible toward your fund specifically, and the two surveys below, read together, say something more textured than "family offices are in" or "family offices are out."

What two family office surveys actually found

UBS's Global Family Office Report 2025, built from its own client base of 317 single family offices across more than 30 markets with average net worth of $2.7 billion, found that offices planning to change their allocation intended to lower private markets from 21% to 18% on average, a shift UBS attributed mainly to a pullback in direct investments.

The 2025 RBC and Campden Wealth North America Family Office Report, surveying 141 North American offices with average wealth of $2.0 billion, found something closer to steady. Private markets held 29% of the average portfolio in 2025, down only slightly from 30% in 2024, and 88% of offices had some private market exposure. Direct private equity was the most popular category for new investment, even as private equity funds remained the largest existing component of the private markets sleeve.

Neither survey is a random sample. UBS surveyed its own clients, and RBC's North American count overlaps with its global total by coincidence, not design. Read together, my take is that private markets aren't being abandoned by family offices as a group, but a meaningful share are trimming at the margin, mostly out of direct deals rather than funds. That's not the same story for every office, which is exactly why the fund-level mandate question still has to be asked one office at a time.

The part I'd actually worry about: succession

The allocation numbers moved a little. The succession number moved a lot. RBC and Campden Wealth found that 69% of the North American family offices they surveyed in 2025 had a succession plan in place, up from 53% the year before. UBS separately put succession planning at just over half of family offices globally.

I don't think most GPs are factoring that in. A relationship built carefully with a founding principal, the person who actually understood the thesis and liked the team, can survive a transition in title just fine, or it can quietly stop mattering the day a next-generation family member or a new CIO takes over the file. Neither survey tells us how often that happens to an existing manager relationship. What it tells me is that the question is live, and it's live more often now than it was a year ago.

Fit still beats wealth and access

None of this changes the underlying test. A great family office, a great fund, and a warm introduction between them still doesn't equal a qualified conversation if the asset class, stage, or check size doesn't line up. Access isn't alignment. The data above just adds two more things worth checking before you treat "family office" as shorthand for "likely yes": whether this particular office is actually growing its private markets book or trimming it, and whether the person you know there is still the person who'll be deciding next year.

Common Questions

Are family offices increasing or decreasing their private equity allocations right now?

Both, depending on which office and which survey you read. UBS found offices that planned changes intended to lower private markets from 21% to 18% on average in 2025. RBC and Campden Wealth found North American family offices held 29% of the average portfolio in private markets in 2025, down only slightly from 30%, with 88% holding some private market exposure and direct private equity as the most popular category for new investment. Both can be true at once. The asset class isn't shrinking, but it isn't a blank check either.

What's the difference between mandate fit and a family office having money?

Money tells you they can write a check. It tells you almost nothing about whether this fund, this asset class, this stage, and this check size are what they're actually set up to invest in right now. A family office with a $2 billion balance sheet can still be the wrong conversation for a $75 million venture fund.

Should I worry about succession at a family office I'm talking to?

I'd at least ask about it. RBC and Campden Wealth found that 69% of the North American family offices they surveyed in 2025 had a succession plan, up from 53% the year before. That's a fast rise in a short window. A relationship built with one principal can outlive that principal's role, or it might not, and I'd rather know which before I'm counting on it.

Is a single family office a better fit for an emerging fund than an institutional LP?

Sometimes, because the decision-maker is often closer to the money and the process can move faster. That's my experience, not a finding from either survey. Neither one measured decision speed or compared family offices against institutions on that question.
