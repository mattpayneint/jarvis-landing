How Do GPs Find Allocators Actively Deploying Capital?

Mandate fit tells you an allocator could write the check. It tells you nothing about whether they're writing any checks right now, and that gap matters more in this market than it used to.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Mandate fit and timing are different questions. An allocator can be perfectly within mandate for your fund and still not be deploying any new capital this year.
  • Bain and Company's Global Private Equity Report 2026 found 53% of LPs say undrawn prior commitments are limiting their new ones, up 15 percentage points from the year before.
  • The same report found distributions have stayed below 15% of net asset value for four years running, essentially flat at 14% most recently, a level last seen around 2008 and 2009. Cash isn't coming back to LPs fast enough for much of it to go back out.
  • Allocators haven't stopped caring. A real share of the conversations GPs are having right now is with people who are genuinely interested and genuinely stuck.
  • Keep qualifying for mandate fit, and add a timing filter on top of it. Treat a not now as a relationship worth keeping, not a dead end.

A GP doing the qualification work right usually asks whether an allocator is within mandate, the right asset class, the right thesis, a check size that makes sense. That's the correct first question. It just isn't the last one. An allocator can check every one of those boxes and still have no capital moving anywhere this year, for reasons that have nothing to do with your fund.

That gap between fit and timing is wider right now than it's been in a while. Bain and Company's Global Private Equity Report 2026 found that 53% of LPs say they're limited in making new commitments because capital already committed to prior funds hasn't been drawn down yet, up 15 percentage points from the year before. The same report found distributions, the cash actually coming back to LPs, have stayed below 15% of net asset value for four years running, essentially flat at 14% most recently, a level the industry hasn't seen since around the 2008 financial crisis. Exits are backed up behind that: Bain counted roughly 32,000 unrealized companies worth about $3.8 trillion still sitting in portfolios, with almost 40% of them held more than five years now, up from 29% in 2019.

That data describes the broader private capital market, mostly institutional LPs, not any single family office or individual allocator. But it's the environment everyone in the market is operating in right now, and it shows up in a specific way: the allocator who was genuinely warm six months ago may be exactly as warm today, and exactly as unable to move.

Mandate fit and timing are two different questions

A $200 billion family office CIO meeting a GP through a friend-of-a-friend introduction, loving the strategy, and still saying "this isn't within our mandate" is the textbook version of a fit problem. The subtler version is more common and easier to miss: within mandate, just not currently allocating. A seed-stage fund fits an allocator's asset class and thesis perfectly, and the allocator is at target allocation for the year, or their pacing plan already points somewhere else. Most qualification processes stop at fit and never ask the timing question at all, so this allocator gets filed next to a real rejection when they're nothing like one.

Net worth or a title tells you an allocator has capital. It doesn't tell you whether that capital is relevant to your fund at this specific moment.

Why so much capital is sitting still right now

The mechanics behind the Bain numbers are straightforward once you see them laid out. LPs commit capital to a fund, that capital gets called down over several years, and eventually the fund returns money through exits, distributions. When exits slow down, the whole cycle backs up. Bain's 2026 report put the exit backlog at roughly 32,000 unrealized companies worth about $3.8 trillion, with holding periods stretching past five years for an increasing share of them. Less money is coming back, so less money is going back out, and the 53% of LPs citing undrawn commitments as a constraint is the direct result.

Bain's own framing of the environment was blunt: fundraising "remained a grind" for a lot of general partners in 2025. Part of that grind is a real no. A good part of it is a real yes stuck behind a timing problem that has nothing to do with your pitch.

The mistake most pipelines make

Most GPs treat every mandate-fit allocator the same way, spending identical effort on someone who just closed a commitment to a similar strategy and someone who told their last three managers to check back next year. The standard fundraising advice, qualify by mandate, stops exactly there, and in a normal market that's close enough. Right now it isn't. With more than half of LPs capacity-constrained by their own prior commitments, treating fit as the whole qualification misses the variable actually deciding whether this quarter's conversations turn into capital.

A new deck or a faster follow-up cadence won't fix this. What fixes it is recognizing that a GP talking to fifty mandate-fit allocators this year is really talking to two different groups, people who can move now and people who can't, and spending the same energy on both is a quiet way to stall a raise.

Build for both answers, not just yes

The practical shift is tracking timing signals the same way you already track mandate fit. A recent commitment to a comparable strategy, visible pacing language, a stated target allocation they're still filling, these are closer to a real answer than anything inferred from title or AUM. When the signal says now, that's where the real urgency belongs. When it says not yet, the relationship still has value, because a lot of that parked capital frees up eventually, on its own schedule, not on a follow-up calendar.

This is also where peer-to-peer relationship building earns its keep. Real capital still moves because two people in the same world got to know each other before either one needed anything from the other, often years before any raise was on the table. Building those relationships ahead of the ask means the timing question gets answered honestly later, instead of under pressure in a first meeting where neither side wants to say "not now" out loud.

Common Questions

How do GPs know if an allocator is actively deploying capital right now?

Rarely from mandate fit alone. Look for a recent commitment to a similar strategy, a stated pacing plan they're still filling, or language about being under-allocated to your asset class. Those are closer to real timing signals than title, net worth, or a warm introduction.

Does mandate fit mean an investor is ready to invest?

No. Mandate fit means they could invest, in theory, and no more than that. Bain and Company's Global Private Equity Report 2026 found 53% of LPs say undrawn prior commitments are limiting their new ones, so a real share of mandate-fit allocators simply aren't deploying right now, for reasons that have nothing to do with your fund.

Why do LPs who seemed very interested never commit?

Usually timing, not interest. An allocator can be genuinely within mandate and genuinely out of room to commit this year because capital committed to earlier funds hasn't come back to them yet. The same Bain report found distributions have stayed below 15% of net asset value for four years running, essentially flat at 14% most recently. Treat the pause as a true answer, not a soft no.

What should a GP do when a good-fit investor isn't deploying right now?

Keep the relationship, lower the urgency, and let the timing question answer itself later. The investors who come back once their capital frees up are usually the ones who were told the truth the first time and never chased.
