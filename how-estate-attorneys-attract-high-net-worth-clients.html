How Estate Planning Attorneys Attract High-Net-Worth Clients

You only ever see the referrals that turned into clients. The family that was given your name and called someone else never reaches your records, so what happens between the referral and the call mostly goes unexamined.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Your records show only the referrals that worked. A family that was given your name and hired someone else leaves no trace in your practice.
  • The one large survey that asked about referrals found plenty of the other kind. In Martindale-Avvo's 2019 survey of 6,300 general legal consumers, only 41% of those who received a lawyer referral said they hired the recommended lawyer, and 32% chose someone else. It did not survey wealthy families.
  • So I'd work on what happens after your name is given: what a family finds when it looks you up, and whether the family already knows you by then.

Most attorneys I talk with treat a referral as the finish line. Someone gives a family your name, and the rest is a formality. The one large survey I can find on referrals to lawyers shows something different for ordinary legal consumers. In Martindale-Avvo's Hiring an Attorney 2019 study, which surveyed 6,300 people, only 41% of those who received a referral to a lawyer said they hired the lawyer who was recommended to them. Another 32% chose someone else.

You only see the referrals that worked

I think that number surprises people because an attorney's picture of referrals is built from the ones that became clients. The family that was given your name, looked you up, and called someone else never shows up in your records. Neither does the family that meant to call and never did. So when an attorney tells me his referrals almost always hire him, I believe that the ones who called did. What neither of us can see is how many times his name was given out and no call came.

You don't need the survey's exact figure to take the point. Whatever your own rate is, your practice has no way of showing it to you. If a CPA gave your name to a family last spring and they hired someone else, would anyone have told you?

It also means attracting wealthier clients has two parts: getting your name mentioned, and being chosen once it has been. Most of the attorneys I work with have only ever worked on the first part.

What the survey covers and what it doesn't

That survey covered general legal consumers. It did not cover families with large estates, and I have not found a study that does. The referrals in it came from any source, the published figures don't give the size of the referred group or say what the other 27% of that group did, and the data is from 2019. So I read 41% as a rough marker for ordinary legal matters. What I say on this page about high-net-worth families is my professional judgment, from twenty years of helping professionals build these relationships.

Between the referral and the call, you get looked up

The same survey gives a hint about what happens in that gap. Of the people who received a referral, 46% checked the lawyer's reviews before making contact, and 45% researched the recommended attorney's or firm's website. The recommendation got the lawyer looked at, and the decision came after the look.

My judgment is that a family with a complicated estate looks at least that hard. So look at what a family finds in the ten minutes after your name comes up. Read your own site as that family would. Does it describe the kind of estate and the kind of family you do your best work for, or could it be any firm's page?

Why I'd get known to the family before anyone refers them

The other way to close that gap is to be known to the family before anyone gives them your name. Estate work tends to arrive in events. A family goes years without needing anything, and then a company sells or a diagnosis comes and they need serious counsel this month, which is when there is the least time to get to know someone new. The engagements my clients value most tend to come from people who had known them for a while before any legal need came up.

The nearest research I have is about a different hire. In a 2023 Dynasty Financial Partners survey of 1,000 investors who each had at least $500,000 to invest and already worked with an advisor, 57% said they had spoken only to the advisor they went on to hire. Dynasty runs an advisor referral service, and the survey reached only people who ended up with an advisor, so I take it as a pointer and no more: many people with money did not compare before choosing.

Referrals are worth keeping, and they shouldn't be the whole plan

In the Martindale-Avvo survey, more people hired the lawyer they were referred to than chose someone else, and I'd keep every referral relationship you have. What I'd question is treating referrals as the whole plan. A referral arrives when someone else happens to think of you, and what the family does with it is out of your sight.

Common Questions

Should estate planning attorneys advertise?

Nothing in the research I've read shows that advertising fails with wealthy families, and nothing shows that it works for estate counsel. My own preference is relationships built peer to peer, because that is what I've spent twenty years on and what I see working for my clients.

Do referrals from financial advisors work for estate attorneys?

Yes, and I'd keep every one. No research I've found measures how often a referral from an advisor or CPA turns into an engagement for an attorney. The one large survey I can find that asked legal consumers about referrals, Martindale-Avvo's in 2019, covered referrals from any source, and the published figures don't separate out professional ones.
