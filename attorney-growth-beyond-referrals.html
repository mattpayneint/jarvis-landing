How Attorneys Grow Beyond Referrals

In a referral-only practice, every new client has first passed through someone else's judgment. The families whose CPA never raised the subject, or gave another name, never reach you, and the surveys I've found show a large share of people finding a lawyer or an advisor without a referral.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • A referral-only practice hears only from the families someone else chose to send. Another professional decided the family needed counsel, when, and whose name to give.
  • A large share of people arrive some other way. Martindale-Avvo reported in 2023 that nearly half of legal consumers looking to hire do not rely on a referral. In FiComm Partners' 2026 survey of 1,000 investors who work with an advisor, 49% of those with $5 million or more said no referral was involved in finding that advisor. Neither studied how wealthy families choose an attorney.
  • I'd keep every referral source and add a second way in that you run yourself: direct relationships with families and business owners, started before they need counsel.

Most attorneys I talk with built the practice on referrals. What I'd look at is who a referral-only practice never hears from. Every prospective client has passed through someone else's judgment first. The CPA decided the family needed an attorney, decided the time was right, and decided yours was the name to give. A family whose CPA never raised the subject, or raised it and gave another name, does not exist as far as your practice is concerned.

Nobody can count those families for your practice. Two surveys give a sense of how common it is to find a professional without being sent.

A large share of people find a professional without a referral

Martindale-Avvo reported in 2023 that nearly half of consumers looking to hire a lawyer do not rely on a referral. Its Hiring an Attorney 2019 study, with 6,300 respondents, found that 43% use referrals as part of their research and 43% also use Google searches.

The only breakdown by wealth I've found is about a different hire. For its 2026 report The New Growth Equation, FiComm Partners worked with Absolute Engagement to survey 1,000 investors who work with an advisor. Among those with $5 million or more to invest, 49% said no referral was involved in finding their advisor, and 50% said a referral was involved. In the two tiers below, the no-referral share was 44% and 43%. That is a gap of a few points between subgroups whose sizes the report doesn't give, so I don't read it as proof that wealthier people lean on referrals less. What it does show is that at the top of that survey, about as many people came without a referral as with one.

What these surveys cover

Neither one tells you how a family with a large estate, or an owner selling a company, chooses counsel, and no study I've found does. The first covers ordinary legal consumers, and Martindale-Avvo published no sample size with its 2023 figure. The second covers investors choosing an advisor, and FiComm doesn't define its referral labels. So I take one modest thing from them: in both surveys, a large share of people arrived without a referral. The rest of this page is my professional judgment.

The dependence tends to show up between year ten and year fifteen

What I see with my clients usually shows up somewhere between year ten and year fifteen. The work is good and the referrals come, and then revenue starts to lurch. A handful of people account for most of the new matters. When one of them retires or merges his firm, the practice feels it for a long time.

Referred clients are often better clients, and I'd keep them

That referred clients are better clients is the objection I hear most, and I think it is often true. A referred client arrives with some trust already in place, and I would keep every referral source you have. What I'd change is the dependence. If every new matter has to begin with someone else remembering you, then the pace of your growth is theirs to set.

The direction I'd take: a second way in that you run yourself

I'd have a way of meeting the right families and business owners that does not depend on being sent. There is more than one way to do that, and nothing I've read shows the others fail. The way I prefer is direct relationships built peer to peer, started well before there is a matter. I've spent twenty years helping professionals do this, over 325 of them.

The objection I would raise in your place is time. You run matters and bill hours, and staying in real conversation with a few dozen people for months is a job you have no room for. That is the part I take on.

Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of high-net-worth individuals who actually want to meet and speak with you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the network and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

The daily consistency runs through Jarvis, my agentic technology, and you take the conversations.

One thing I'd think hard about before starting is who, specifically. A relationship with a family that will never need the work you do best costs the same hours as a relationship with one that will. Most of the value comes from choosing well at the start, and that choice is different for every practice. It's the first thing I'd want to work through with you.

Common Questions

What's the best marketing for estate planning attorneys?

The research doesn't name one. No study I've found covers how wealthy families choose estate counsel, and the general-consumer data shows people using referrals and online research together. My preference is direct relationships built peer to peer, because that is what I see working for my clients.

Do wealthy clients respond to law firm content marketing?

I have no data on wealthy clients specifically. For general legal consumers, Martindale-Avvo's 2022 research with more than 3,000 people found that nearly 70% use online content such as profiles and reviews to understand their legal issue. That figure is about profiles and reviews, so it doesn't show that a firm's own articles get read, and nobody has measured the question for families with large estates.
