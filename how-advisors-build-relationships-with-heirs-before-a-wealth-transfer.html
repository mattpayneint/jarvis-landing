How Advisors Build Relationships With Heirs Before A Wealth Transfer

A lot of advisors meet the kids for the first time at the worst possible moment, right after a parent passes. By then, trust has to start from zero. Here's how to be a known quantity well before that day ever comes.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Households routinely leave when the wealth passes to the next generation, and the reason is almost always the same: the heir never actually knew the advisor.
  • Meeting the kids once at a holiday dinner isn't a relationship. It's a name they vaguely recognize when the money moves, which isn't enough to keep them.
  • Waiting for the parents to make the introduction depends on their memory and their timing, neither of which an advisor controls.
  • The fix is a direct, peer-level relationship with the heir, years before the transfer, built with no agenda attached to the first conversation.
  • Done right, the household stays because the trust was already there, not because anyone scrambled to build it after the fact.

Here's a pattern I've watched play out with advisor after advisor. A client passes, the estate settles, and the advisor who managed that family's money for twenty years meets the adult children for the first time at exactly the moment they're deciding who handles it next. The advisor has decades of trust with the parents and none with the people actually making the decision now.

The money usually doesn't stay. Not because the advisor did anything wrong managing it. Because the relationship that mattered, the one with the parents, simply doesn't transfer to the next generation on its own. Trust isn't inherited. It's built, one relationship at a time, with whoever actually holds the decision.

The handoff that rarely survives on its own

Most advisors have felt this one personally. A patriarch or matriarch who trusted you completely passes away, and within a year or two the household's assets have moved to a different advisor entirely, usually one the adult children already knew from their own life, their own circle, their own trust network. It's not disloyalty. It's just how trust actually works. People move their money to whoever they already believe in, and a parent's advisor they've met twice at a birthday party rarely clears that bar.

This is the quiet reason a lot of books of business shrink over a decade even while the advisor does everything right for the clients still at the table. The attrition isn't a service problem. It's a relationship gap that opens up one generation at a time, and it's almost entirely preventable.

Why waiting on the parents doesn't work

The obvious fix sounds simple: ask your clients to introduce you to their kids. Some will. Most won't think of it, or the timing never feels convenient, or the kids live somewhere else and the opportunity never quite comes up. You're depending on someone else's memory and someone else's schedule to protect a household you've spent years building. That's the same referral problem advisors already live with in their own peer network, just one generation removed and higher stakes.

A direct relationship, one you build yourself with the heir as a person rather than as an extension of the parents' account, doesn't have that dependency. It happens on your timeline, with no one else's memory required.

What building it early actually looks like

Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of high-net-worth individuals who actually want to meet and speak with you, including the next generation of the households you already serve and families you haven't met yet. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the network and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

The approach stays peer to peer. Nobody is being pitched, and nothing about it treats the heir as a future account instead of a person. You're simply getting to know them, years ahead of any transfer, the same way you'd get to know any other relationship worth having. When the day eventually comes that the wealth does move, you're not meeting them for the first time. You're already someone they know. The daily consistency behind building and keeping up with that many relationships runs through Jarvis, my agentic operator, while you spend your time with the clients in front of you.

Why this protects the whole book, not just one household

Chris, a VP at Merrill, landed $60M in new AUM off a single conversation using this same relationship-first approach. The mechanics are identical whether the person across the table is a prospect you've never met or the adult child of a client you've had for fifteen years. Trust gets built the same way either time: peer to peer, no pitch, real interest in them as a person.

Advisors who treat next-gen relationship building as part of the practice, not an afterthought, end up with households that compound across generations instead of resetting to zero every time a decision-maker changes. That's the real asset. Not one great client. A family that stays.

Common Questions

How do financial advisors build relationships with heirs before a wealth transfer?

By meeting the next generation years before the transfer, as a peer, with no agenda attached to the first conversation. Most advisors only meet the heirs after the parents are gone, which is the hardest possible moment to build trust from zero. Advisors who keep the household started the relationship long before that.

Why do so many heirs leave their parents' advisor?

Usually because there was no relationship to keep. The parents trusted the advisor. The kids, if they'd ever met at all, met briefly at a holiday dinner. When the money moves, it moves to whoever the heir already trusts, and a stranger with a long history with their parents isn't automatically that person.

Can I just ask my clients to introduce me to their children?

You can, and sometimes it works, but it depends entirely on the parents remembering to do it and the timing being convenient for them. That's the same referral problem advisors already have with their own peer network, just one generation over. A direct relationship you build yourself doesn't depend on anyone else's memory.

Isn't it premature to court someone who hasn't inherited anything yet?

Not if it's framed as a real relationship instead of a pitch. Nobody wants to feel like they're being positioned for a future inheritance. The advisors who do this well build a genuine, peer-level connection with the heir as a person, years before any transfer, with nothing asked of them in return.
