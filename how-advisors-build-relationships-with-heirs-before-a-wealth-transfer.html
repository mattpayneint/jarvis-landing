How Advisors Build Relationships With Heirs Before A Wealth Transfer

The usual deadline for meeting the kids is the transfer. I think there's an earlier one: the day the heir first hires an advisor of their own, which can come years before any inheritance.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Just one in five affluent investors use the same advisor as their parents, according to Cerulli research from the fourth quarter of 2023.
  • The parents' advisor was rarely even considered. Among affluent investors who use their own advisor, more than 90% did not consider their parents' advisor.
  • So I think the date that matters is the heir's first hire. Cerulli didn't measure when these investors chose, so the timing is my reasoning and not a finding.
  • The relationship has to exist before then, and it has to be about the heir's own financial life. I'd build it on purpose for the households that matter most to the book.

Here's the version of this problem most advisors carry around. A client passes, the estate settles, and the adult children move the money to someone they already know. So the plan becomes meeting the kids before that day comes.

I think the instinct is right and the deadline is off. An heir who already has an advisor of their own chose that advisor at some point, and in the Cerulli research the parents' advisor was rarely considered when they did. If that choice came before the inheritance, as I suspect it often does, then the inheritance was not where the decision got made.

Cerulli asked affluent investors about their parents' advisor, and its summary doesn't give the sample size or define affluent. It wasn't a survey of people who had inherited, so when I write heirs I mean your clients' adult children. It counts which advisor people use and whether they considered their parents'. It doesn't measure money leaving after a death, and it doesn't say when anyone chose. I haven't found a reliable figure for either, so the timing argument on this page is mine. The Dynasty survey I use further down comes from a firm that runs an advisor-matching service, and it covers investors with at least $500,000 who already had an advisor.

The parents' advisor was rarely considered

The Cerulli Edge, U.S. Retail Investor Edition for the fourth quarter of 2023, reported that just one in five affluent investors use the same advisor as their parents. Among affluent investors who use their own advisor, more than 90% did not consider their parents' advisor, and just 6% gave that advisor even the slightest consideration.

The second figure is the one I'd pay attention to. Most of these investors didn't weigh their parents' advisor and then pick someone else. For most of them the parents' advisor never came up, so there was nothing for that advisor to win or lose.

An inheritance hasn't been shown to trigger a change

A 2018 study in the Journal of Financial Planning looked at who changes their financial planner and what predicts it. The authors reported that no evidence was found to support the common perception that an inheritance triggers a change, and they called prediction very difficult. It was a survey of 312 people and not a wealthy sample, so it is a pointer and not proof. I take it to mean the estate settling shouldn't be assumed to be the moment of decision.

The date that matters is the heir's first hire

If an heir hires a first advisor at 35, the best chance to be considered came at 35, no matter when the parents' estate eventually settles. In a 2023 Dynasty Financial Partners survey of 1,000 investors, a specific life event prompted 54% of those aged 35 to 44 to seek advice, and 42% of those under 35. A large share of the younger investors in that survey went looking because something happened in their own lives, and it may have had nothing to do with their parents.

In the same survey, 57% said the advisor they hired was the only one they had spoken to. If heirs choose the way those investors did, whoever they already know when their own moment arrives is likely to be the one advisor they talk to.

That raises a question about your own practice. If a client's daughter got married or sold some company stock this year and wanted advice, would she think of you? And if she called, would your minimum let you take her? An advisor whose practice can't serve the heir today has, without meaning to, sent her to find the advisor she may still have when the inheritance arrives.

Heirs don't have the assets yet

That's true, and an advisor's time is limited, so I wouldn't tell anyone to pursue every client's children. I'd make it a choice. Pick the households where losing the next generation would hurt the book most, and build those relationships as if the heir were the client, on the heir's own questions and at the heir's own stage of life. Nobody wants to be cultivated for a future inheritance, and people can tell when they are.

Common Questions

Do heirs really leave their parents' advisor?

I haven't found a number for that I'd trust. Figures for how much inherited money leaves get repeated a lot, and I haven't seen one with a source solid enough to pass along. What Cerulli measured in the fourth quarter of 2023 is narrower: which advisor affluent investors use, and whether they considered their parents' advisor. That's why I talk about being considered and leave attrition rates alone.

Can I just ask my clients to introduce me to their children?

Yes, and sometimes that's all it takes. In my experience it depends on the parents' memory and timing, so I wouldn't make it the whole plan. What matters is that the heir ends up with a relationship with you that holds up without the parents in the room.
