How Do Advisors Find Clients Going Through a Divorce or Remarriage?

Most advisor marketing chases the moment an inheritance lands. One academic study found no evidence that moment changes who manages the money. A change in marital status did.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • The common assumption is that an inheritance is the moment a family starts shopping for a new advisor. A 2018 study in the Journal of Financial Planning found no evidence to support that.
  • What the same study did find: respondents who were remarried, widowed, or divorced had changed at least one financial planner at a rate of 74.4%, against 50.9% for everyone else in the sample.
  • The sample was general consumers with $25,000 or more in household income, not a study of wealthy households, so I read it as a pointer, not a precise figure for high-net-worth families.
  • So I'd watch for marital transitions, not just liquidity events or inheritances, as the moment a financial relationship is actually more likely to change hands.

A lot of the advice advisors hear about life events points at the same moment: someone inherits money, and that's when you're supposed to be standing there. I've given versions of that advice myself. A 2018 study in the Journal of Financial Planning looked directly at what predicts whether someone switches financial planners. Inheritance didn't turn out to be the answer. A change in marital status did, by a wide margin.

The study surveyed 312 usable respondents through an online panel in late 2015, with a household income floor of $25,000. That's a general population, not a study of wealthy households, and I haven't found research that repeats this specific question at the high-net-worth level. I'm citing it because it's the clearest finding I know of on what actually predicts a planner switch, and because what it found runs against the advice I hear most often in this business.

The inheritance moment is weaker evidence than it looks

The study's authors wrote plainly that they found no evidence to support the common perception that an inheritance triggers a planner change, and called prediction in this area very difficult generally. That's a strong statement from people who went looking for the connection and couldn't find it. Meanwhile remarried, widowed, or divorced respondents had changed at least one planner at 74.4%, against 50.9% for the rest of the sample, a real and sizable gap in the data they actually had.

I don't think this means inheritances are irrelevant. The wealth transfer coming over the next two decades is enormous by any measure, and advisors are right to stay close to it. This study just answers a more specific question: what moves a relationship. On that question, money arriving on its own wasn't enough. A change in the household did the work.

What this study actually measured

I want to be direct about the limits here, because the figure is easy to overstate. This was a broad consumer sample, not a study of six or seven figure portfolios, and I haven't found this same question asked of wealthy households specifically. So I'm treating the direction of the finding, marital transition over inheritance, as the useful part, not the exact percentages as a forecast for next quarter's new clients.

Why a marital transition moves the relationship more than money moving does

This is my own reasoning for why the pattern holds up, separate from what the study itself measured. An inheritance usually lands in an account somebody is already managing. The balance grows, the household doesn't, and whoever held that relationship keeps holding it. A divorce or a remarriage rebuilds the household itself. New accounts open. A name comes off a joint account and goes onto one of its own. The person who used to sit in the review meeting may not be the one who owns the money anymore.

That's a structural reason for a new advisor to end up in the picture, not simply a bigger balance for the old one to manage. The person now holding the accounts may never have been in the room for the original relationship, which makes this less a comparison between advisors and more a fresh start.

The honest objection: isn't this just another liquidity event?

A fair pushback is that I already treat business sales and other liquidity events as moments to build a relationship ahead of, so why does a marital transition deserve its own page. The difference is how the event behaves. A sale has a timeline a CPA or banker can usually see coming for a year or more, and it ends with the same person still owning the money, just more of it. A divorce moves on a court's schedule, not a bank's, and it can end with someone who was never the primary decision-maker managing an account alone for the first time. There's rarely a public announcement to wait for, the way there often is with a sale.

Where I'd build the relationship, and when

I wouldn't try to find out who's getting divorced. That's not a list anyone should want to be working from, and it would cost you more trust than it's worth even if you could get it. What I'd build instead are real peer relationships with the professionals who sit closest to that moment before it's public: family law attorneys, divorce mediators, and the CPAs who end up preparing the first separate tax return. Those professionals see a marital transition coming well before an advisor would.

The goal isn't a referral arrangement where names get traded for a fee. It's getting known to those professionals as a peer, well before either of you has a reason to send anyone anywhere, so that when the moment actually comes, your name is one that's already familiar rather than one that has to be found.

Common Questions

Does this mean advisors should approach people going through a divorce directly?

Not the way that sounds. A person in the middle of a divorce is not a lead, and reaching out cold around something that private would cost you more trust than it could ever buy. What I'd build instead is a relationship with the professionals who already sit next to that moment, so your name is already known to the household before anyone has to go looking for it.

How would an advisor even know a prospect is getting divorced before it becomes public?

Mostly you wouldn't, and I wouldn't try to find out. The people who do know early are family law attorneys, divorce mediators, and the CPAs who end up filing the first separate return. Building real peer relationships with that group is a slower, more honest path to the same timing.

Should advisors stop focusing on wealth transfer and inheritance content?

No. Cerulli projects that around $124 trillion will transfer through 2048, and that's too large a shift to ignore. But the study I'm citing is about something else, whether receiving an inheritance by itself predicts a planner switch, and it found no evidence that it does. I wouldn't treat an inheritance alone as the trigger. I'd treat wealth transfer as a long relationship to be in early, and marital transition as a separate, faster-moving signal.

Is remarriage really as significant a signal as divorce?

The study groups remarried, widowed, and divorced respondents together as one category of marital status change, so it doesn't break remarriage out on its own. What I take from it is the broader point: when a household's marital structure changes, the financial relationship is more likely to be re-examined than when the household stays the same and money simply arrives.
