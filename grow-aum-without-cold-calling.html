How to Grow AUM Without Cold Calling

Most of what a new client costs an advisor is the advisor's own hours. So I'd judge every way of growing, cold calling included, by whose hours it uses.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Most of what a new client costs is your own time. A Kitces report on advisor marketing, published in 2020, put the cost of acquiring a client at $3,119, of which $2,600 was advisor time.
  • That holds for the comfortable alternatives too. In the same report, centers of influence such as CPAs and attorneys cost $9,144 per client, more than paid advertising, because of the hours.
  • I can't tell you cold calling fails with wealthy households. I haven't found a study that measures it either way.
  • My preference is a network of relationships you chose, built peer to peer, with the hours of research and follow-up taken off your plate.

Let me say something for cold calling first. A lot of very good advisors built their first book on it, and the discipline it teaches is real. I haven't found a study that measures how it performs with wealthy households, for or against, so I'm not going to tell you it can't work.

My case for growing another way starts with a different number. In the best data I know on advisor marketing, most of what a new client cost was the advisor's own hours. Dialing uses those hours, and so do most of the things advisors do instead of dialing. So the question I'd ask of any method is whose hours it runs on.

That data is a Kitces report on how financial planners market their services, published in 2020. It surveyed advisors of every kind, so none of its figures are specific to wealthy households, and they are old enough that I read them for proportion and not as today's prices. It doesn't measure cold calling at all. Everything past the figures is my judgment from working with advisors.

The hours are the cost

The report put the overall cost of acquiring a client at $3,119. Only $519 of that was hard dollars. The other $2,600 was the advisor's time. By channel, paid advertising came to $3,805 per client, networking to $4,494 and centers of influence to $9,144.

That last figure deserves a second look. Building relationships with CPAs and attorneys feels free because nobody sends an invoice, and in this data it was the most expensive of the three per client, because of the hours. That doesn't make it a poor use of time, since a client who costs more to win can be a much larger client. It does mean the price of relationship work is paid in hours, and those hours come out of the same week as client service.

This is where cold calling and its more comfortable alternatives share a weakness. Dialing takes your hours. So does lunch with every CPA in town. Trading one for the other doesn't fix growth if you are still the person doing all of it between client meetings.

The report doesn't pick a winning method

The same report compared first-year revenue for every dollar spent, and it gives the top 25% and the bottom 25% of advisors separately. Referrals were the most efficient channel it lists for both groups, at 19X for the top and 1.7X for the bottom. Paid web listings returned 8.3X for the top group and 0.4X for the bottom, so one channel covered a very wide range of results. If the report sorted advisors by those results, part of that gap is built in, and I wouldn't claim it shows what caused the difference. What I take from it is narrower. No channel in that data comes with a result attached, and that includes whatever you'd replace cold calling with.

Advisors were most satisfied when the leads were good ones

One more line from that report matters here. Advisors' satisfaction with their marketing was, in its words, "most correlated to the quality of leads." A good lead and a household that fits your practice aren't quite the same thing, so the next step is mine. In twenty years I've seen that an advisor will gladly do hard things for the right household and will resent easy things that fill the calendar with the wrong ones.

A 2023 Dynasty Financial Partners survey is the other reason I'd narrow the list. Of 1,000 investors with at least $500,000 who already had an advisor, 57% said the advisor they hired was the only one they had spoken to. Dynasty runs a service that matches investors with advisors, so allow for its interest in the subject. If that many people talk to one advisor, I'd rather spend the hours becoming the advisor a well-chosen family already knows than making first calls to strangers.

If cold calling is working for you

Then nothing in this data says to stop. My case for doing something else is narrower. Most advisors I've worked with dislike dialing and do it inconsistently for that reason. I've also watched trust form more easily with people who have money when the first contact isn't a request. Both of those are my experience, and I don't have a study for either.

Count your hours as the cost, then build the network on purpose

I'd start by treating hours as the cost of every growth activity. Before adding one, ask what it takes from your week and which parts of it don't need you: the research on who is worth knowing, the keeping in touch, the follow-up that slips when a busy quarter hits. Then I'd narrow the group you're trying to know to households that fit your practice.

The part I'd build on purpose is the network itself, which is the work I do with advisors. Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of high-net-worth individuals who actually want to meet and speak with you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the network and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

It's peer to peer, so nobody is being pitched or chased, and your book stays selective. The research and the daily follow-up run through Jarvis, my agentic technology, which is how the hours get covered without you becoming a marketer. You keep the conversations, which are the part you're good at.

Chris, a VP at Merrill, landed $60 million in new AUM off a single conversation that came out of this kind of relationship building. That is one advisor's result, and yours will depend on you.

Common Questions

How do advisors grow AUM without buying leads?

Purchased lists did better in the research than their reputation suggests. The Kitces report published in 2020 shows them returning 7.2X first-year revenue per dollar for the top 25% of advisors, although advisors were less satisfied with them and the data isn't specific to wealthy households. For some advisors buying a list may be the right call. If you'd rather not, the alternative is a network of relationships you build yourself, with families chosen to fit your practice. It takes longer to start.

Does content marketing work for reaching wealthy clients?

It can be how a family first finds you. In a 2023 Dynasty Financial Partners survey of 1,000 investors with at least $500,000 who already work with an advisor, 23% said they found their advisor through social media, a blog or another online source. Respondents under 45 relied less on referrals and were about three times as likely to use online sources. That figure covers a broad group of online sources and tells you how people found an advisor, so it doesn't measure how well content marketing works. My view is that content helps a family get comfortable with you, and the relationship still gets built in conversation.
