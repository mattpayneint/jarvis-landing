How Advisors Get Referrals That Match Their Ideal Household

Referrals feel like the safest way to grow. The data says the richer the household you're targeting, the less likely a referral brought them in at all, and even the referrals you do get aren't screened for fit.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Referral involvement actually falls as the target household gets wealthier. A 2026 FiComm Partners and Absolute Engagement study found 49% of investors with $5 million or more invested had no referral involved in choosing their advisor, versus 43% to 44% at lower wealth tiers.
  • A referral that does happen is not automatically the right household. "My friend needs an advisor" doesn't check whether that friend is the right size, complexity, or life stage for your practice before the meeting gets booked.
  • Among investors who do credit a referral, a 2023 Dynasty Connect survey found 46% came from a friend, family member, or colleague, and 23% from a professional advisor such as an accountant or lawyer.
  • Cerulli's 2025 advisor survey found centers of influence, including CPAs and attorneys, account for 13.9% of new clients, a real source but a distant second to personal referrals.
  • Keep working referrals, and build on top of them. Deliberate relationships, especially with the professionals already inside your target households' lives, get you further than waiting for the right referral to show up on its own.

Every advisor I talk to treats referrals as the gold standard, and for good reason. A referred prospect usually arrives trusting you before you say a word. What gets missed is that referrals get less common, not more, the richer the household you're trying to reach, and even when one shows up, it was never screened for whether that household actually fits your practice.

A 2026 research report from FiComm Partners and Absolute Engagement, surveying 1,000 investors already working with an advisor, found referral involvement drops as wealth rises. Among investors with $5 million or more invested, 49% said no referral was involved in choosing their advisor at all, compared with 44% at the $1 million to $4.9 million level and 43% at $500,000 to $999,000. The richer the prospect, the less likely a referral did any of the work for you.

That doesn't mean referrals stop mattering. It means the growth plans built mostly around referrals are, by definition, built around a mechanism that thins out right where the biggest households live.

The referral you want is not always the referral you get

When a client sends a friend your way, they're vouching that you're good, decent, worth a conversation. They're not checking whether that friend's $300,000 household matches a practice built for $3 million relationships, or whether the friend is in a life stage your team actually serves well. Most of the time they're not thinking about fit at all, because it never occurred to them that fit was a variable.

That's how a practice ends up with a calendar full of consults that go nowhere. Nobody did anything wrong. Nobody along the way was screening for whether this was the right relationship at this moment, only whether it was a trustworthy one.

Where referrals actually come from, and why that matters

Dynasty's 2023 Connect survey of 1,000 advised investors found 46% were referred by a friend, family member, or colleague, 23% by a professional advisor such as an accountant or lawyer, and 23% through social media, a blog, or another online source. Cerulli's 2025 advisor-side data, from a separate sample, found personal referrals account for over half of new clients at 54.2%, with centers of influence second at 13.9%. The two numbers aren't directly comparable since one is what investors remember and the other is what advisors report, but they point the same direction: personal referrals are the biggest channel, professional referrals are a real second channel, and neither one was built to filter for your ideal household.

The professionals who already sit inside your best households

The CPAs and attorneys who show up in that 13.9% are worth more attention than the number suggests, because they already sit inside the exact households an advisor is trying to reach. A CPA doing a business owner's taxes, or an estate attorney drafting a trust for a $10 million family, has a closer view of that household's actual complexity and timing than any friend-of-a-friend ever will. The advisor-attorney-CPA triangle is slow and often lopsided in practice, each professional waiting on the others to think of them first, but it's the one referral channel that comes closest to pre-screening for the household you actually want.

Building that relationship on purpose, rather than hoping it develops naturally over years of birthday cards and holiday emails, is the difference between a channel that happens to you and one you can actually plan around.

Build the part of growth you can actually control

Referrals still matter. The problem is building the whole growth plan around a mechanism that thins out right at the top of the market and was never designed to check for fit in the first place. The advisors with the most predictable growth write down exactly who their ideal household is, what they're worth, how complicated their situation is, what stage of life they're in, and then build relationships deliberately with that profile and with the professionals who already serve it, instead of waiting for the right referral to wander in.

Common Questions

Do referrals still work for financial advisors chasing bigger households?

They still happen, but less reliably the richer the target household gets. A 2026 FiComm Partners and Absolute Engagement study found 49% of investors with $5 million or more invested had no referral involved in finding their advisor at all, the highest share of any wealth tier measured in that study.

Why doesn't a referral always mean a good fit?

Because nothing about a referral conversation screens for fit before the meeting happens. A friend recommending an advisor is vouching for trust, not for asset level, complexity, or whether the household matches the practice's ideal client. The intentions are good. The screening just isn't there.

How much of advisor growth actually comes from centers of influence?

A real amount, but a distant second to personal referrals. Cerulli's 2025 advisor survey puts centers of influence, the category that includes CPAs and attorneys, at 13.9% of new clients. Dynasty's 2023 investor survey found 23% of advised investors credited a professional advisor specifically.

What should advisors do instead of just waiting on referrals?

Build direct, peer-to-peer relationships with the households and the professionals around them who already match the ideal client profile, rather than leaving growth to whoever happens to remember to refer. It's the same relationship-building instinct behind a good referral, done on purpose instead of by chance.
