How Estate Attorneys Build Relationships With CPAs And Advisors

The research on financial advisors shows that professionals like you are a known source of their clients, and that advisors spend heavily in their own hours on those relationships. Most attorneys I meet approach an advisor as if only the attorney had something to gain.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Advisors get clients through professionals like you. In a 2023 Dynasty Financial Partners survey of 1,000 investors with $500,000 or more, 23% said they were referred to their advisor by a professional such as an accountant or lawyer.
  • They pay for those relationships in their own hours. A Kitces Report published in 2020 put an advisor's cost of acquiring a client through a center of influence at $9,144, against $3,805 through paid advertising, because of advisor time.
  • So I'd come as a peer with something to offer, choose a few advisors and CPAs whose clients fit your practice, and aim to be the second name they think of.

Most estate attorneys I work with tell me their best clients came through a CPA or a financial advisor, and most of them describe the flow the same way: a good quarter, then months of nothing. When I ask how they approach those relationships, it usually sounds like someone hoping to be remembered. There's a lunch once a year, and then a lot of waiting.

The research on advisors made me see those relationships differently. In 2023, Dynasty Financial Partners surveyed 1,000 investors who each had at least $500,000 to invest and worked with an advisor. Asked how they found that advisor, 23% said they were referred by a professional advisor such as an accountant or lawyer. That is the investors' own account, and it doesn't mean attorneys and CPAs decide who gets hired. It does mean that, for an advisor, professionals like you are a known source of clients.

Advisors already put real time into knowing people like you

Advisors report something similar from their side. In Cerulli Associates' U.S. Advisor Metrics 2025 report, released in February 2026, advisors said centers of influence, a group that includes CPAs and attorneys, were their second most common source of new clients at 13.9%, behind referrals from clients, friends and family at 54.2%. That is advisors' own report across all their clients, so it is a different measure from the 23%.

What caught my attention is what advisors spend on those relationships. A Kitces Report on advisor marketing, published in 2020, put the cost of acquiring a client through centers of influence at $9,144, compared with $3,805 through paid advertising, and advisor time was the reason. The data is several years old and isn't broken out by profession, so I wouldn't quote it as today's cost of knowing an attorney. It does tell you that advisors were putting a lot of their own hours into these relationships.

The same report suggests those hours don't pay off for everyone. From centers of influence, advisors in the top quarter brought in first-year revenue of 7.8 times what they spent, and advisors in the bottom quarter brought in 0.8 times, which is less than they put in. If the report sorted advisors by that result, some gap between top and bottom is built in, so I don't make much of its size. What I take from it is narrower: for the bottom quarter, these relationships had not paid for themselves in the first year. So my guess is that the advisor across the table from you is in one of two places. Either these relationships work for him and he looks after the ones he has, or he has sat through years of lunches that went nowhere and is tired of them.

The research stops at the advisor's side

All of that describes advisors. I found no credible number on how often an advisor or a CPA refers a family to an estate attorney, on what makes them choose one attorney over another, or on what CPAs spend on relationships like these. That an advisor has a business reason to know you is my reading of those figures. How to approach him, and the CPA, is my professional judgment from what I see with my clients.

Come as a peer, without a request

What I see is that the relationships producing steady introductions are the ones where the attorney shows up as a peer with something to contribute. The ones that stall usually began with a request. Asking an advisor outright to send you clients puts him on the spot. Given what he already spends on these relationships, you don't have to come as the one asking.

If he already has an attorney, aim to be the second name

Most advisors already have an attorney they send people to, in my experience, and I wouldn't try to displace that person. What I see is that an advisor's usual attorney fits most of his clients and not all of them. One family's estate has outgrown the usual attorney's practice. Another has a business succession that needs someone who does that work every week. Being the second name an advisor thinks of, for the matters his usual attorney doesn't fit, is a better position than it sounds.

What I'd do: a few relationships, chosen for fit, kept up steadily

I'd stop spreading thin across every advisor and CPA in town. I'd choose a few whose clients look like the families you do your best work for, and I'd treat each one as an ongoing professional relationship where you compare notes on what you're each seeing. There is no request attached, and nobody owes anybody an introduction.

The hard part is keeping it up. Staying in touch with a dozen people for a year while you run matters is where this usually falls apart, and it's the part I take on.

Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of CPAs, financial advisors, and the high-net-worth families around them who actually want to know you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the relationship and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

The daily consistency runs through Jarvis, my agentic technology, while you do the work only you can do.

Which advisors and CPAs, and what you bring to the first conversation, depends on your practice. That is the conversation I'd want to have with you.

How do estate attorneys build relationships with CPAs and financial advisors?

The research I've found covers the advisor's side only, and it shows advisors spending a good deal of their own time on centers of influence, a group that includes attorneys and CPAs.

Is this different from traditional networking?

Mainly in how narrow and how steady it is. Traditional networking is a few events a year with whoever is in the room. This is ongoing contact with a small number of CPAs and advisors, chosen because their clients match your practice.
