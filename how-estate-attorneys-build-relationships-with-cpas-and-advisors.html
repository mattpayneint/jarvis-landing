How Estate Attorneys Build Relationships With CPAs And Advisors

CPAs and financial advisors see a family's full financial picture long before a lawyer ever enters the room. Here's how to become the attorney they already think of, instead of the one they forget to call.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • CPAs and advisors are the real gatekeepers for estate work. They know a family's numbers, their complexity, and their urgency before an attorney ever hears about it.
  • Waiting for a referral to come back around is not a relationship. It's hope dressed up as a strategy, and it grows your practice at the speed of someone else's memory.
  • The attorneys with the deepest referral flow built it deliberately, peer to peer, with specific CPAs and advisors whose clients match their ideal estate, long before any file ever changed hands.
  • You don't need to replace their favorite attorney. You need to be the second name they think of for the matters their usual referral doesn't fit.
  • The relationship comes first. The referrals follow on their own. Nobody has to ask for anything.

Every estate attorney I've worked with says some version of the same thing: most of their best clients came through a CPA or a financial advisor. And almost every one of them also admits the flow is unpredictable. A great quarter, then three months of nothing, with no real explanation for either.

Here's the part worth sitting with. The CPA preparing a family's tax return, or the advisor managing their accounts, knows things about that family's situation long before the attorney does. The business sale that's coming. The second marriage that complicates the beneficiary forms. The parent who just passed and left a mess. They see it first, every time. Which means the entire referral relationship is downstream of something you don't control unless you build it on purpose.

Why CPAs and advisors see the family first

Think about the order things actually happen in. A family sells a business, inherits money, or starts planning for a parent's decline, and the first call almost always goes to whoever already has their trust: the CPA doing their taxes, or the advisor managing their portfolio. The estate attorney shows up third, if at all, and only if the CPA or advisor happens to think of one at the right moment.

That's not a knock on attorneys. It's just the order of operations in a wealthy family's financial life. Which means the fastest way to meet more of the right families isn't advertising to them directly. It's being the attorney already in the mind of the people who talk to them first.

The referral relationship most attorneys actually have

Here's the honest version of most CPA and advisor relationships I've seen: reciprocal in theory, lopsided in practice. One side remembers to send something over, the other side doesn't, and nobody ever quite closes the loop. It's relationship-heavy and slow, and it stays that way forever unless someone treats it like a real relationship instead of a professional courtesy.

The attorneys who get consistent flow from CPAs and advisors did one thing differently. They stopped waiting for the annual bar mixer to be the whole strategy, and started having real, ongoing conversations with specific people whose client base actually matches their ideal estate. Not a pitch about sending business. Just two professionals who respect each other's work, talking regularly about what they're each seeing.

What this looks like in practice

Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of CPAs, financial advisors, and the high-net-worth families around them who actually want to know you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the relationship and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

Nobody is being pitched or asked for anything. The conversations are peer to peer, professional to professional, which is exactly the register CPAs and advisors already operate in. Over time, when the right family situation comes up on their end, your name is simply there, already trusted, with no awkward ask required on either side. The daily consistency behind building and maintaining that many relationships runs through Jarvis, my agentic operator, while you do the work only you can do.

Why the second-name position is worth more than it sounds

You almost never need to be anyone's first call. CPAs and advisors already have a go-to attorney for the matters that fit cleanly. What they rarely have is a second name for the files that don't, the complex business succession, the blended family, the estate that's grown past what their usual attorney wants to take on. Attorneys who position themselves there, deliberately, end up with a steady flow of exactly the matters they want, without ever competing head-on for anyone's incumbent relationship.

None of this requires anything that would look to a compliance department like solicitation on anyone's behalf. It's two professionals getting to know each other, the same way the best referral relationships have always started, just built on purpose instead of left to chance.

Common Questions

How do estate attorneys build relationships with CPAs and financial advisors?

Directly and deliberately, as a peer, before any referral is needed. Most attorneys meet a CPA or advisor once at a bar event and hope something comes of it. The attorneys with the deepest referral relationships treat those professionals the way they'd treat a future client: real conversation first, business later.

Should I just ask CPAs and advisors to send me referrals?

Asking outright almost always backfires. It puts the other professional on the spot and reads as needing their help more than they need yours. A real relationship, built over time, makes the referral obvious without ever having to ask for it.

What if a CPA or advisor already has a favorite attorney?

Most do, and that's fine. Their favorite handles the files that fit. Your job is becoming the second name they think of, for the matters outside their usual attorney's comfort zone or capacity. That second-name position fills up a calendar faster than most attorneys expect.

Is this different from traditional networking?

The difference is intent and consistency. Traditional networking is a handful of events a year, hoping something sticks. This is a steady, purposeful relationship with the specific CPAs and advisors whose client base actually matches your practice, built week over week instead of left to chance encounters.
