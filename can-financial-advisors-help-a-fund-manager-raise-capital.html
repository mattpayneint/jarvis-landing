Can Financial Advisors Help a Fund Manager Raise Capital?

Most GPs build their LP list out of family offices and institutions and never think about financial advisors, even though advisors already route a large, growing sum into exactly this kind of allocation.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Advisors are already a real, measured channel into private markets. Cerulli Associates reported in September 2025 that U.S. financial advisors allocate $1.9 trillion to less-liquid private market strategies, projected to reach $3.7 trillion through 2029.
  • Most advisors say it helps their own practice. A 2023 Cerulli survey found 67% of advisors agree private market investments help them attract high-net-worth clients.
  • They also name real friction. The same survey found liquidity, client education, and complexity and diligence are the barriers advisors cite most, in that order.
  • None of this promises a check into any one fund. I'd still treat advisors as a peer relationship to build early, the same as any other allocator, not a shortcut.

Ask most GPs who they're trying to reach and you'll hear family offices, maybe an institution or two, sometimes a handful of angels. Almost nobody says financial advisors. I understand why. An advisor isn't the one writing the check from their own balance sheet, and a wealth manager doesn't fit most people's mental picture of an LP.

The numbers say that picture is out of date. Cerulli Associates, in a press release dated September 30, 2025, reported that U.S. financial advisors currently allocate $1.9 trillion to less-liquid private market strategies, and projected that figure to grow to $3.7 trillion through 2029. That number comes from a research firm counting what's already happening in advisor books, with a specific projection for where it's headed.

Advisors say this helps them, not just that they tolerate it

A separate Cerulli survey, conducted with Invesco and the Investments and Wealth Institute and published in a press release dated September 27, 2023, found that 67% of advisors agree private market investments help them attract high-net-worth clients, 66% agree it helps them consolidate and retain assets under management, and 81% agree it differentiates their practice. That survey predates 2024 and doesn't state its sample size, and agreement with a statement isn't the same as behavior. Still, these aren't reluctant advisors describing something forced on them. They're describing something they want more of.

The honest objection: agreement isn't allocation

The strongest case against building toward advisors is that agreeing a strategy helps your practice is cheap, and actually allocating client money into a specific fund is not. The same 2023 Cerulli survey found that half of advisors report an alternative allocation of 5% or less, so for most of them this is still a small part of the book. And a 2025 Goldman Sachs Asset Management survey of institutional LPs found that commitments are concentrating with managers LPs already have relationships with, fewer but larger checks to names they already know. I'd expect something similar inside advisor books. An advisor who's never heard of your fund isn't suddenly moving 5% of a client's account into it because a projection in a research report said the category is growing.

That's also why relationship building matters here, the same way it matters with any other allocator. The $3.7 trillion doesn't arrive at your fund on its own. It arrives at the funds the advisor already trusts enough to recommend.

What advisors say is actually in their way

The 2023 Cerulli survey also asked advisors what holds them back from allocating more to private markets. Lack of liquidity was named most often, at 56%. Client education came next, at 44%, and product complexity and due diligence followed at 39%. Those are the three things an advisor has to feel comfortable explaining to a client before they'll move money, and none of them are solved by a pitch deck arriving cold.

My read is that a manager who's already spent real time with an advisor, well before asking for anything, is the manager that advisor can actually explain to a client when the moment comes. A stranger pitching the liquidity terms, the structure, the diligence, gets nowhere. Someone the advisor already trusts gets heard.

Treat it like any other allocator relationship, started early

I wouldn't build a raise around advisors instead of family offices or institutions. I'd add them as a channel most GPs are ignoring, and build the relationship the same way I build any other one in this vertical: peer to peer, well before there's a fund to discuss. The advisor's own friction points, liquidity, education, complexity, become things you help them get comfortable with over time, not things you ask them to overlook.

Is the advisor channel actually growing, or is that just a projection?

It's a projection, and I'd treat it as one.
