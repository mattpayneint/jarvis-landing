How Sponsors Find LPs Within Their Investment Mandate

A deal-by-deal sponsor is rarely short of wealthy people to call. What's scarce, in my experience, is the handful whose own criteria fit this deal, at this size, right now. I'd find them by starting from the deal, and I'd keep a record of every investor who says no.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Fit is the hard part, and wealth alone doesn't narrow much. A list sorted by net worth tells you who has capital and nothing about what they're set up to invest in.
  • The closest research is about funds. A 2025 study by Shang and Leifeld of Preqin commitment data found that shared regional, strategy and industry preferences raised the likelihood of a commitment between an LP and a GP. It covers institutional LPs, and it shows who transacted without showing why.
  • I'd start from the deal and work back to the investor, removing people at each step until the ones left have a real reason to talk with you. That's my framework, and no study stands behind it.
  • Build the relationships before the deal is live, peer to peer, and keep a record of every no. A no tells you where an investor's mandate ends.

A sponsor raising one deal at a time has a narrower problem than a fund manager, and a shorter clock. There's one opportunity, with its own asset type, structure, check-size range and closing date, and the investors who fit it are a small group.

They are a small group inside a very large one. Capgemini's World Wealth Report 2025 counts 7.9 million high-net-worth individuals in the U.S., where high net worth means at least $1 million in investable assets outside a primary residence. I use that number only for scale, since it doesn't say how many of those people could or would invest in a private deal. Finding people with money is the easier part of the job, and finding the ones who fit is where the time goes.

The closest research is about funds, and it is consistent with starting from fit

I haven't found research on how deal-by-deal sponsors and their investors find each other. The closest I can point to is about funds. In a 2025 paper in Social Network Analysis and Mining, Shang and Leifeld modeled commitments between 7,669 LPs and 7,153 GPs in Preqin's database from 2016 to 2021, across private equity, real estate, private debt, natural resources and infrastructure. Shared regional, strategy and industry preferences raised the likelihood of a commitment, with diminishing returns.

That is a pattern in who ended up transacting among institutional LPs and funds. It doesn't show that any investor screens on mandate fit, and it doesn't cover single deals, family offices or individuals. The same limit applies to the Goldman Sachs survey I cite further down. So read the rest of this page as my framework and my experience. That one study is consistent with it and doesn't prove it.

Wealthy isn't the same as within mandate

Take a family office with $500 million. It can be right for one opportunity and wrong for the next. It may only write checks above $5 million, or not do direct real estate at all, or be fully allocated to your asset class for the year, which is a timing problem and has nothing to do with how they feel about you. None of that shows up on a list sorted by net worth.

A sponsor doing deal-by-deal raises doesn't have a fund mandate, but the investor still has one, whether it's written in a policy statement or carried in the principal's head. A great investor, a great deal, and a warm introduction between them still come to nothing if the structure, the check size or the timing doesn't line up. Access without alignment is still just a nice conversation.

Start from the deal and work back to the investor

I'd run the search as a chain: opportunity, investor mandate, thesis alignment, check-size capacity, structure, timing, and then a real reason for a conversation. It's my framework, built from client work.

It starts with stating your own deal as plainly as you'd want an investor to state their criteria: the asset type, the vehicle, the check-size range that makes sense, and where you are on the clock. Then each link removes people. Whoever is left at the end is someone you have a rational reason to know, and who has one to know you. I'd sooner start there than with the question of who has money.

Fit is eligibility, and the deal still has to earn the check

The pushback I'd expect is that investors care about the sponsor's record and the terms far more than any of this. I think that's right. Goldman Sachs Asset Management's 2025 Private Markets Diagnostic Survey found that LPs prioritize fees and terms, track records, and team stability, and I have no reason to think individual investors care less about who they're handing money to.

Mandate fit doesn't compete with any of that. Within mandate means an investor could do this deal. Whether they want to depends on the deal and on you. The chain doesn't improve the deal. It puts the deal in front of investors who are able to do it.

Build the list before the deal is live, and keep a record of every no

In my experience, a deal's clock is short, and trust takes longer than most deal timelines allow. So the relationships have to exist before the deal does. I'd build them around the kind of deal you do, peer to peer, with no offering on the table, and learn each investor's criteria in the course of ordinary conversation.

One habit I'd add: write down why each investor passes. An investor who says they don't go below $5 million, or that they're done for the year, has told you where their mandate ends. After a few deals, you know which of your relationships fit which kind of opportunity, and the next raise starts from a list built on real answers.

How you're allowed to communicate about a live offering depends on your exemption, and that's a decision for your securities counsel. I'm not a lawyer.

Todd, a friend running a fund, landed $35 million for his bourbon fund building relationships this way, around a defined thesis, well before he needed the money. Pete, a client of mine, landed a $100 million investor the same way. I can't promise anyone the same result, and two raises is a small sample.

Common Questions

What's the difference between mandate fit for a fund and for a deal-by-deal sponsor?

A fund's mandate governs a whole strategy across many deals. A sponsor doing deal-by-deal raises doesn't have a fund mandate, but the investor still has one. The question becomes whether this specific opportunity, with this asset, this structure and this check size, fits what that investor is trying to do right now.

Should a sponsor target investors by net worth or AUM?

Only as a first filter. Wealth tells you someone has capital and leaves their criteria unknown. After that I'd look at what the investor is set up to do: asset class, structure preference, check-size range and timing.

How early should a sponsor start building investor relationships?

Before the deal is live. In my experience, a deal's closing timeline is shorter than the time it takes an investor to get comfortable with a sponsor they've just met, and an investor who already knows you, and whose criteria you already know, can decide when a fitting opportunity shows up. I can't cite a study on how long that takes, so I'd start as early as you can.
