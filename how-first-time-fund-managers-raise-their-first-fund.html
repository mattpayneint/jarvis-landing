How Do First-Time Fund Managers Raise Their First Fund?

Everyone says they love the strategy, then the conversation goes quiet. The deck usually isn't the problem. What's missing is a real signal, not a sharper pitch.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Raising a first fund is hard mainly because of what outsiders can't see. Nobody outside the fund can judge your skill directly, so they watch for a signal instead.
  • Research on venture investors found that existing backers learn about skill firsthand, while everyone else reads the absence of that backing as a quiet verdict.
  • Capital is also concentrating in fewer, bigger funds every year, so the old playbook of conferences and warm intros gets harder for a new entrant with each cycle.
  • The fix is having a real relationship with a few likely investors already in place before you ever ask anyone to go first, not a sharper pitch.
  • What it actually takes is building the relationship before you need the signal. A bigger name or a longer track record won't substitute for that.

When a first-time manager can't get traction, the instinct is to blame the deck, the warm intro that never converted, or the stage you're raising at. I hear a version of the same question from almost every first-time GP I talk to: why does everyone say they love the strategy, then go quiet. The strategy is usually fine. The problem sits one level up, in what a stranger is actually being asked to do when they consider writing a check to a fund with no track record behind it.

The signal nobody tells you about

Academic research on venture capital explains this better than instinct does. Hochberg, Ljungqvist, and Vissing-Jorgensen, writing in the Review of Financial Studies in 2014, found that a fund's current investors learn directly whether a manager has skill, while everyone else can only observe returns and watch who else is already in. Outside investors treat the absence of existing backers as a sign of low skill, even when it isn't one. That gives a manager's existing investors real say over the next fund, and it means a brand new fund with zero investors is carrying the heaviest version of that same problem. The hard part isn't convincing people your strategy works. It's asking a stranger to be the very first data point, with nothing yet to read except your own word for it.

Why the old playbook is getting tougher, not easier

In 2024, just over half of all committed capital went to the 100 biggest funds, according to Preqin's State of Private Capital Fundraising in 2025, up from more than a third in 2021. Bain's 2026 Global Private Equity Report described the broader fundraising environment as a continuing grind, noting that the number of funds closing fell for a fourth straight year, in almost every category. That doesn't mean the opportunity to raise a first fund has closed. It means the old route into it, conference season and secondhand introductions, is competing for a shrinking share of attention each year. That's one more reason to build a different route on purpose instead of waiting on the old one.

The first believer doesn't have to be big

One real relationship that existed before the ask breaks the signal problem, no matter the size of the check behind it. I've watched this play out at very different scales. Todd built his bourbon fund's first real backer this way, a relationship that existed long before any number was ever discussed. Pete, a client of mine, landed a $100M investor the same way: the relationship came first, the capital followed once backing him had already become an easy conclusion rather than a leap. That person becomes the signal every other investor will eventually look for.

What this looks like in practice

Before you open a raise, build genuine peer relationships with the handful of people whose mandate and stage actually fit this fund: family offices, angels, and individual investors already active in your space, not a wide net of anyone with money. The conversations start as two people in the same world comparing notes, not a pitch, so there's nothing yet to sell them on. By the time you're ready to raise, a few of those people already know what you're building and have been watching it take shape. That's the signal a stranger would otherwise have to take purely on faith, already in place before you need it.

Common Questions

Why is it so hard for a first-time fund manager to raise capital?

Mostly because outside investors cannot see your skill directly, so they watch for a signal instead, usually whether other investors have already backed you. Research on venture investors found that a fund's existing backers learn about skill firsthand while everyone else reads the absence of that backing as a quiet warning. A brand new fund carries the heaviest version of that problem, since there is no backing yet to read.

Does a first-time fund manager need an anchor investor before raising?

An anchor helps, but it does not need to be institutional or large. What matters is that the relationship existed before the ask, so the first believer feels like a conclusion rather than a gamble. I have seen that work with a single real relationship built well before any number came up, with the same effect as a bigger name writing the first check.

Is it getting harder for emerging managers to raise their first fund?

The data points that way. Preqin reported that just over half of all committed capital in 2024 went to the 100 biggest funds, up from more than a third in 2021, and Bain's 2026 report described fundraising broadly as a continuing grind with the number of funds closing falling for a fourth straight year. Capital is concentrating, which makes the old conference and introduction playbook harder for a new entrant with each cycle.

How do first-time fund managers build LP relationships without an existing network?

By starting the relationship before the raise, not during it. Real conversations with the handful of people whose mandate and stage actually fit the fund, peer to peer, long before any ask. By the time the raise opens, a few of those people already know what is being built, and backing it is a conclusion they reached on their own instead of a pitch they have to evaluate cold.
