How Do Financial Advisors Get Centers of Influence to Refer Clients?

Most advisors treat their CPA and attorney relationships as a nice-to-have that occasionally sends someone over. The data on how clients actually found their advisor tells a different story.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Advisors report that centers of influence, CPAs, attorneys, and other professionals, are a real but modest source of new clients, well behind personal referrals.
  • Clients report it differently. Investor surveys show a meaningfully bigger share crediting a professional referral than advisors themselves report receiving.
  • The gap is wide enough to matter. It suggests the center of influence channel is already doing more work than most advisors give it credit for, usually because it's managed casually instead of deliberately.
  • High-net-worth households are also more likely to have an advisor who actively coordinates with their CPA and other outside professionals. That relationship seems to matter more the wealthier the household gets.
  • The fix is building the actual relationship first, the same way you'd build one with any other peer, not asking CPAs and attorneys for referrals more often.

Ask most advisors about centers of influence and you'll get a shrug. A CPA sends someone my way every year or two, nothing I'd call a strategy. That's a fair read of a relationship that was never actually built on purpose. But the data on how clients say they found their advisor doesn't match the data on how advisors say they got them.

A number that doesn't match what clients say

The Cerulli Report's 2026 study of advisor organic growth found that personal referrals from clients, friends, or family account for just over half of new clients, 54.2 percent, with centers of influence the second most common source at 13.9 percent. But when Dynasty Financial Partners asked investors themselves how they found their advisor, in its 2023 Dynasty Connect survey, 23 percent said they were referred by a professional advisor, for example an accountant or lawyer. Those are two different samples, advisors reporting their own book and investors reporting their own history, so the numbers aren't a before-and-after of the same group. But the gap is wide enough to notice: clients give professional referrals more credit than advisors seem to be claiming.

Why this matters more at the high end, not less

Research published in the Journal of Financial Planning in 2025 by Sommer and Lutter found that among high-net-worth respondents, advisors were more likely to be described as coordinating with CPAs and other outside advisers than advisors serving less wealthy households were. The study didn't measure whether that coordination wins new clients, only that it shows up more often in high-net-worth relationships. Either way, it points at something worth noticing: the wealthiest households seem to expect their advisor is already talking to the other professionals in their life. If that relationship has never actually been built, it's a piece these households are already looking for and not finding.

What this looks like in practice

Treat the CPA or attorney like any other peer worth knowing, with real conversations about what each of you is seeing in your respective practices, a consistent rhythm instead of a once-a-year check-in, and genuine interest in their business as well as yours. Handled that way, the referral becomes the natural result of a relationship that already exists, instead of a favor either side has to think twice about.

Common Questions

How do financial advisors get centers of influence to refer clients?

By building a real relationship with the CPA, attorney, or other professional first, not by asking for referrals on a schedule. The Cerulli Report's 2026 organic growth study found centers of influence account for 13.9 percent of new advisor clients, the second largest source after personal referrals, which means the channel already works when the relationship is real.

Do centers of influence actually produce new clients for advisors?

Advisors report them as a smaller share than clients do. Cerulli's 2026 research put centers of influence at 13.9 percent of new advisor clients, while Dynasty Financial Partners' 2023 investor survey found 23 percent of respondents said a professional advisor like an accountant or lawyer referred them. The gap suggests this channel is doing more work than most advisors realize.

Why don't CPA and attorney referrals happen more often for advisors?

Usually because the relationship was never built deliberately in the first place. A once-a-year check-in reads as a transaction to a busy CPA or attorney, rather than a relationship worth prioritizing. The advisors who get consistent referrals treat the professional like any other peer worth knowing, with a real rhythm instead of an annual ask.

How do advisors build relationships with CPAs and attorneys the right way?

The same way you'd build one with a client worth having. Real conversations, consistency over time, and genuine interest in their practice. Research from the Journal of Financial Planning in 2025 found that advisors serving high-net-worth households were more likely to coordinate with CPAs and other outside advisers, which suggests this kind of relationship grows in importance as a household's wealth grows, rather than fading.
