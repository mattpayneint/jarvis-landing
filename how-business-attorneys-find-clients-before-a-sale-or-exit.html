How Business Attorneys Find Clients Before a Sale or Exit

By the time there's a term sheet, the owner has usually already decided, without ever running a search, whose call to take. Getting into that circle after the deal is live is harder.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Deal counsel usually comes from a circle the owner already trusts, not a search the owner runs once the deal starts. CPAs, bankers, and other attorneys are a real part of that circle.
  • But that circle is a modest channel on its own. Cerulli's U.S. Advisor Metrics 2025 report found centers of influence, the category CPAs and attorneys fall into, account for 13.9% of new clients for financial advisors, second behind personal referrals.
  • High-net-worth households lean on that coordinated circle more than most. Research published in the Journal of Financial Planning in 2025 found households with $1 million or more were more likely to say their adviser coordinates with CPAs and other outside advisers than less wealthy households were.
  • So I'd work on getting into that circle years before a deal, not during one. Relationships with owners and the CPAs and bankers around them, started early, are what put you in the room when the clock starts moving.

Most of the business attorneys I talk with wait for the call. A CPA they know mentions a client who's thinking about selling, or an owner they met at a chamber event finally picks up the phone once a buyer shows real interest. By then the attorney is reacting to a process that's already moving, with other professionals already attached to the deal.

I don't think that's usually how it actually happens. My experience, after twenty years working with professionals who sell trust for a living, is that the person who ends up as deal counsel is almost always someone the owner, or someone close to the owner, already knew before the deal was real. That relationship is usually in place years earlier. The sale just puts it to work.

The referral channel is real, and it's smaller than it feels

Cerulli Associates' U.S. Advisor Metrics 2025 report, released in February 2026, found that personal referrals from clients, friends, or family account for 54.2% of new clients for financial advisors, and that centers of influence, a category that includes CPAs and attorneys, are the second most common source at 13.9%. That report is about financial advisors, not business attorneys, and I haven't found an equivalent study for legal clients. I cite it because it's the clearest measurement I know of for how much weight a professional referral actually carries, and 13.9% carries real weight without being the main source. If that pattern holds anywhere close to true for attorneys, a practice built entirely on CPAs and bankers remembering your name is leaving most of the opportunity on the table.

Wealthy households run on a more connected circle of professionals

A study published in the Journal of Financial Planning in March 2025, surveying decision-makers with $250,000 or more in investable assets, found that respondents with $1 million or more were more likely to say their financial adviser coordinates with their CPA and other outside advisers than respondents below that threshold. The study didn't measure trust directly, and it says nothing about attorneys specifically or about business sales.

It matches what I see with my own clients: at the level of wealth where a business sale actually produces a meaningful outcome, the professionals around that owner already talk to each other more than they do at lower wealth levels. That's the circle a business attorney wants to already be inside, well before anyone is thinking about a term sheet.

Why waiting for the deal to start is the expensive way to do this

Once a sale process actually begins, it moves on the buyer's timeline, not the owner's, and there's rarely room to vet a new attorney from scratch. The owner reaches for someone already known, even if that someone is only a name their CPA mentioned once in passing. An attorney with no prior relationship to the owner, or to the professionals around the owner, starts that race already behind. Rather than waiting to be the name someone else happens to give out, I'd build the relationship directly with owners who might sell someday, and with the CPAs and bankers who'll see it coming before the owner says a word publicly.

Common Questions

Does a referral from a CPA or banker guarantee a business attorney gets the deal?

No, and I wouldn't treat it that way. Cerulli Associates' U.S. Advisor Metrics 2025 report found that referrals from centers of influence, the category CPAs and attorneys fall into, account for 13.9% of new clients for financial advisors, the second largest source behind personal referrals. That's a real channel worth keeping, it just isn't the whole plan. A CPA thinking of you is one thing. A CPA calling you the week a deal starts moving is another.

How early should a business attorney start building relationships with owners who might sell someday?

Years before, in my experience. I haven't found research that measures how far ahead of a sale the eventual deal counsel relationship starts, so this is my professional judgment, not a study. Owners move fast once a deal is live, and the attorney already in the circle has a big head start over one trying to get noticed once the process starts.

Is this a solicitation rules problem?

How you market to prospective clients yourself is governed by your own state's bar rules, and that's a question for your own counsel, not me. What I work on is relationship building, which is a different thing than solicitation of a specific matter.

Should business attorneys still chase CPA and banker referrals?

Yes, and I'd keep every one. I'd just stop treating them as the only door in. The data on how wealthy households find a financial advisor shows a meaningful share arrive with no referral involved at all. I'd expect something similar on the business side, so it's worth building a second way in yourself, alongside the referrals.
