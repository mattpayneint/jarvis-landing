Do Financial Advisors Need More Prospect Meetings, or Better Ones?

The same marketing channel returned 19 times what one group of advisors spent, and less than what another group spent. Whatever separates those two outcomes, it isn't how many meetings either one booked.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • The same channel performs wildly differently by advisor. A 2020 Kitces report on financial planner marketing found referrals returned 19X first-year revenue per dollar for the top 25% of advisors, and 1.7X for the bottom 25%.
  • Advisors reported quality mattering more than cost. The same report found satisfaction was most correlated to the quality of leads, not the price paid per client.
  • At the wealthiest tier, referrals only cover about half the field. A 2026 FiComm Partners survey found 49% of investors with $5 million or more said no referral was involved in finding their advisor.
  • So I'd put less effort into generating more activity, and more into who that activity is actually reaching.

Most of the advisors I talk with describe a thin calendar as a volume problem. More referrals, more leads, one more seminar on the books. The two datasets below suggest the volume isn't where the real gap is. One is advisor-wide and pre-2022, and I'll flag exactly where it stops applying to high-net-worth households specifically.

The same channel, two very different results

The Kitces Report on how financial planners actually market their services, published in 2020, tracked first-year revenue per dollar spent by channel, split between the top 25% of advisors by result and the bottom 25%. For referrals, the top quartile returned 19X. The bottom quartile returned 1.7X.

Channel Top 25% of advisors Bottom 25% of advisors
Referrals 19X 1.7X
Centers of influence 7.8X 0.8X
Paid web listings 8.3X 0.4X

That's not a gap between channels. It's the same channel, referrals, producing very different results depending on who's running it. The report ranks advisors by their actual results, so part of that spread is probably built into the ranking itself. I'd still take the direction of it seriously: picking the right channel matters less than most advisors assume, and how well it's worked matters more.

Advisors said the same thing when asked what satisfied them

The Kitces report also measured advisor satisfaction with each channel, and found it was most correlated to the quality of leads, not the cost per client. Overall client acquisition cost in that dataset averaged $3,119 per client, and centers of influence were the most expensive channel at $9,144 per client because of the advisor time involved, not hard dollars. Advisors weren't happiest with the cheapest source. They were happiest with the source that produced the right fit.

That's consistent with the quartile gap above. A cheap source of plenty of meetings and an expensive source of fewer, better ones aren't the same thing, and the data suggests advisors already feel that difference even when the per-client cost says otherwise.

At the top of the market, referrals cover about half the field

A 2026 FiComm Partners and Absolute Engagement survey of 1,000 investors who work with an advisor found that among those with $5 million or more investable, 50% said a referral was involved in finding their advisor, and 49% said no referral was involved at all. At $1 million to $4.9 million, the no-referral share was 44%. At $500,000 to $999,000, it was 43%.

Referrals probably get more credit in this business than this figure supports, and I'd keep every one a practice has. What the 49% tells me is that a practice built entirely around waiting for referrals is, by design, only working half of the wealthiest tier of households. The other half found their advisor some other way, and the survey doesn't say how.

Build for fit before the meeting, not more meetings after

Wealth alone doesn't tell you fit. A $50 million household can be the wrong prospect for a practice built around $1 million to $5 million relationships, the same way a smaller household can be the wrong fit for a practice built around complexity. The piece worth building deliberately is the filter that happens before anyone's calendar gets a meeting on it: is this household the right size and complexity, is something actually happening in their life right now, and is there already enough trust that the first real conversation doesn't start from zero.

Bottom line, we're essentially creating new networks of high-net-worth individuals who actually want to meet and speak with you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the network and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly. It's peer to peer, which is also what keeps a book selective instead of just busier.

Common Questions

Does spending more on leads fix a thin calendar?

Not by itself. The 2020 Kitces report on financial planner marketing found that advisor satisfaction was most correlated to the quality of leads, not the cost per client. Overall client acquisition cost in that dataset averaged $3,119 per client, with centers of influence running the most expensive at $9,144 because of the advisor time involved. Spending more on a channel doesn't automatically buy better fit. That report is pre-2022 data and isn't specific to high-net-worth households.

Why do top advisors get so much more out of the same channel?

I don't know for certain, and the report doesn't say either. The Kitces data ranks advisors by their actual results, so some of the gap is probably built into how the ranking works. What I take from it is that the channel itself isn't the deciding factor. Two advisors running the same referral program can get wildly different outcomes, which points at fit and follow-through more than at which channel they picked.

Do referrals still matter for high-net-worth prospects?

Yes, and I'd keep every one you get. A 2026 FiComm Partners and Absolute Engagement survey of 1,000 investors found that among those with $5 million or more investable, 49% said no referral was involved in finding their advisor, versus 44% at $1 million to $4.9 million. Referrals cover roughly half the field at the top of the market, which still leaves the other half to someone else.

Is this data specific to high-net-worth households?

Only partly. The FiComm survey on referral involvement is specifically broken out by wealth tier, including $5 million and up, so that figure applies. The Kitces acquisition cost and channel return figures cover advisors of every kind and aren't broken out by the wealth of their clients. I've been clear above about which is which.
