Do Clients Leave Their Financial Advisor After a Spouse Dies?

Conventional wisdom treats a spouse's death as the moment a household starts shopping for a new advisor. In the research, almost the opposite happens, and the real risk to an advisor's book sits somewhere else entirely.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Most surviving spouses keep the incumbent advisor. Cerulli's Trust in Transition research, published in September 2024, found 85% of advised investors stayed with their existing advisor after a spouse died, 91% of men and 81% of women.
  • A generation later, the opposite is true. A separate Cerulli report found that just one in five affluent investors use the same advisor as their parents, and that more than 90% of affluent investors never gave their parents' advisor any real consideration.
  • Some couples already decide this together. Cerulli's Q3 2024 research found 23% of women and 20% of men said their advisor was chosen through a family referral or a spouse's or parent's choice.
  • The real risk is a marriage where only one partner was ever actually part of the relationship. It's not the death of a spouse itself.

If you asked most advisors what happens to a client relationship after a spouse dies, I think a lot of them would guess it's fragile. The surviving spouse is grieving, maybe wasn't the one who handled the money, and other advisors are circling with sympathy cards and a pitch. That's the story I hear most often, and the research doesn't back it up.

The spousal transition isn't the opening it looks like

Cerulli Associates' research report Trust in Transition, published in September 2024, found that among advised investors, 85% stayed with their incumbent advisor following the death of a spouse, 91% of men and 81% of women. The report doesn't state its sample size, so I'd treat the exact figure as directional rather than exact. But even allowing for that, 85% is a strong signal in one direction. The idea that the death of a spouse routinely sends a household looking for someone new just doesn't match this.

I think the reason this surprises people is that it's easy to imagine the moment from the outside: a widow or widower, suddenly alone with decisions they didn't use to make, an obvious target for a competitor. What the research shows is that the relationship the household already had usually absorbs the transition instead of breaking under it.

A generation later, the same relationship mostly doesn't survive

Compare that to what happens when wealth passes to the next generation instead of a spouse. A separate Cerulli Edge report, from the U.S. Retail Investor Edition published in the fourth quarter of 2023, found that just one in five affluent investors use the same advisor as their parents, and that more than 90% of affluent investors who use their own advisor never gave their parents' advisor any real consideration. Only 6% gave it even the slightest thought.

Neither Cerulli report defines "affluent" the same way, and neither states a sample size, so I wouldn't stack the two figures as a single precise comparison. But the direction is hard to miss. One transition keeps the relationship intact almost every time. The other loses it almost every time. Same industry, same basic event, the transfer of wealth after someone's life ends, and two very different outcomes.

Why the difference, in my experience

Neither Cerulli report explains the gap directly, so this is my own reading of it, not a finding either report makes. A separate Cerulli survey, from the Retail Investor Edition published in the third quarter of 2024, found that 23% of women and 20% of men said their advisor was chosen through a family referral or a choice made by a spouse or parent. That tells me a real share of couples treat the advisor relationship as something they both own, not something one of them delegated to the other.

What I see with my own clients is that the households where both spouses were genuinely part of the relationship, not just copied on the statements, are the ones that stay intact when one of them is gone. The surviving spouse isn't meeting the advisor for the first time at the worst moment of their life. A child inheriting a parent's account is usually meeting that advisor for close to the first time, with no relationship behind the name on the statement at all. That's the actual variable, not the transition itself.

What this means for the household you haven't fully met

If that reading is right, a special campaign aimed at recent widows isn't the move, the data suggests they're a weak target anyway. The real fix is making sure both people in a relationship are actually in the relationship as a matter of course: on the calls, in the planning conversations, instead of just the one whose name happens to come up first. The same habit that protects a book when a spouse dies is the habit that gives a household a reason to stay a generation later, when the real risk shows up.

Common Questions

Should advisors specifically target widows and widowers as prospects?

I wouldn't build a prospecting plan around it. Cerulli's Trust in Transition research found 85% of advised investors stayed with their existing advisor after a spouse died, 91% of men and 81% of women. If that holds anywhere close to true, a surviving spouse is one of the least available prospects you could chase, not one of the most.

Does the same loyalty hold when wealth passes to the children instead of a spouse?

No, and the gap is wide. A separate Cerulli report found that just one in five affluent investors use the same advisor as their parents, and that more than 90% of affluent investors who use their own advisor never gave their parents' advisor any real consideration. Spousal transitions and generational transitions behave very differently.

Who usually decides which advisor a couple uses?

Cerulli's Retail Investor Edition for the third quarter of 2024 found that 23% of women and 20% of men said their advisor was chosen through a family referral or a choice made by a spouse or parent. That's a meaningful share of couples where the decision was already a joint one, or someone else's, well before any transition.

What should an advisor actually change based on this?

Make sure both members of a couple are genuinely part of the relationship, not just the one who happens to take the calls. In my experience, that's the real variable behind the gap between the 85% who stay after a spouse dies and the much smaller share who stay a generation later. The households that lose their advisor are usually the ones where only one person was ever really in the room.
