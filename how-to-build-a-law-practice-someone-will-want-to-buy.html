How Do You Build a Law Practice Someone Will Actually Want to Buy?

You tell business-owner clients to build something that doesn't depend on them. Most attorneys never ask the same question about their own practice, and it shows up the day they try to sell it.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • A referral doesn't transfer the way attorneys assume. In Martindale-Avvo's 2019 Hiring an Attorney survey of 6,300 legal consumers, only 41% of people who received a referral to a lawyer actually hired that lawyer, and 32% hired someone else.
  • If a direct, personal recommendation doesn't guarantee loyalty to one named attorney, a practice built entirely on the founder's own relationships is carrying more personal, untransferable value than most owners realize.
  • Cerulli projects $124 trillion will transfer to heirs and charity through 2048, with high-net-worth and ultra-high-net-worth households making up more than half that volume from just 2% of households. That's a long runway of relationship work worth owning, not renting from one person's memory.
  • So I'd build the referral and client relationships as a system the practice owns, not a Rolodex that leaves when the founder does.

I've sat across the table from a lot of attorneys who spend their careers advising business-owner clients on exactly this problem: build something that can run, and sell, without you standing in the middle of it. I'd guess most of those same attorneys have never turned that advice on their own practice.

The reason it matters now is simple. At some point almost every practice either gets sold, merged, handed to a younger partner, or wound down. What a buyer or a successor is actually purchasing in any of those situations is the expectation that clients and referral sources keep showing up after the person they know is no longer the one answering the phone.

A referral is weaker evidence of loyalty than attorneys assume

Martindale-Avvo's Hiring an Attorney study, published in 2019 from a survey of 6,300 legal consumers, found that among people who received a referral to a lawyer, only 41% said they hired the lawyer they were referred to, and 32% said they chose someone else. That's general consumer data, not a study of wealthy families or business owners specifically, and I haven't found a version of it that is. But the direction of the finding is worth sitting with regardless of exactly who was surveyed: even a direct, named recommendation to one specific attorney doesn't reliably produce loyalty to that attorney.

If that's true for a recommendation made directly to a specific name, it's a reasonable bet that it's at least as true for a vaguer kind of loyalty, the kind a CPA or advisor feels toward "my friend's firm" after the friend retires or steps back. What that loyalty was really attached to, in my experience, is the person who answered the phone for twenty years, not the name painted on the door.

Most practice value is sitting in one person's head

I don't think this is controversial once it's said out loud, which is part of why it gets so little attention. Ask most attorneys who their best referral sources are, and they can name them from memory. Ask whether anyone else at the firm could call those same people cold and get the same response, and the answer is usually no. The relationship, and the trust behind it, exists in one person's Rolodex, not in the practice as an institution.

That arrangement works fine for decades, right up until the day it has to be sold, merged, or handed to someone else. At that exact moment, the one thing a buyer most wants to believe, that clients and referral sources will keep calling, is also the hardest to prove and the most likely to be wrong.

The honest objection: isn't a strong personal reputation exactly what makes a practice valuable?

It does, right up until a transition happens. A founder's reputation is real and it's earned, and I'm not arguing against building one. The objection worth taking seriously is that a buyer isn't purchasing the founder's reputation at all, since the founder is usually the one leaving. A buyer is purchasing a bet on what continues without that person in the room. Reputation that lives entirely in one person's relationships is a strong asset for that person and a weak one for anybody buying the practice from them.

Build the relationship engine the practice owns, starting now

The fix isn't a succession memo written the year before retirement. It's building the referral and client relationships differently from the start, so the practice, not just the founder, is the thing the CPAs, advisors, and families actually know. That means more than one person at the firm in real, ongoing contact with referral sources, and a documented, deliberate way those relationships get maintained instead of living in one partner's calendar and memory.

Given how much wealth is set to move over the next two decades, and how much of it concentrates in a small share of households, this isn't a small market to be building that kind of durable relationship engine in. The practices that own this as a system, rather than renting it from one person's personal network, are the ones worth buying when the time comes.

Common Questions

Does a law practice's value really come down to anything besides revenue?

Revenue matters, but a buyer is really paying for revenue they believe will keep showing up after the founder leaves. Two practices with identical revenue can be worth very different amounts if one of them depends entirely on the founder's personal relationships and the other has a client and referral engine that runs independent of any one person.

How do you tell whether referral relationships are institutional or personal?

A rough test I use: if the founding partner took a year off, would the CPAs and advisors who send families still know who to call? If the honest answer is that everything runs through one person's memory and one person's relationships, that's a personal asset wearing the practice's name.

Isn't this just succession planning, which most firms avoid anyway?

It overlaps with succession planning, but I'd frame it earlier and more broadly than that. Succession planning usually starts when someone is a few years from retiring. What I'm describing is a way of building client and referral relationships from day one that happens to also make the practice easier to sell, transfer, or share with a successor whenever that day comes.

Does this apply to a solo estate planning attorney, or only larger firms?

I'd say it applies more to a solo practice, not less. A larger firm already has more than one partner a client might know. A solo practice is, by definition, one person's relationships, which is exactly the structure that's hardest to hand to a buyer or a successor without building something alongside it.
