How Do Attorneys Get Fewer, Better-Qualified Consults Instead of a Fuller Calendar?

A referral gets your name said out loud. In the one large study that measured what happened after that, most referrals didn't turn into a hire. That changes what's actually worth building toward a fuller calendar.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • A referral is a mention, not a hire. In Martindale-Avvo's 2019 survey of 6,300 legal consumers, only 41% of people who got a lawyer referral actually hired that lawyer, and 32% chose someone else.
  • Once someone starts deciding, it moves fast. A later Martindale-Avvo survey found 55% of legal consumers decide within two weeks, and nearly 1 in 4 decide in three days or less.
  • Plenty of people aren't working from a referral to begin with. The same survey found nearly half of consumers looking to hire an attorney don't rely on a referral at all.
  • So I'd stop chasing more referrals and more consults, and work on being the name a family already trusts before that fast window ever opens.

Most of the attorneys and CPAs I talk with describe their growth problem the same way: too few consults on the calendar, and too many of the ones that do show up going nowhere. The instinct is to ask referral sources for more, or to add a channel that produces more inquiries. The research I can find on how legal consumers actually behave points somewhere else.

Everything cited here is general consumer legal research. None of it was built on wealthy families or business owners specifically, and I haven't found a study that was. I'll say plainly where the data stops and my own judgment starts.

A referral gets your name said. It doesn't get you hired

In Martindale-Avvo's Hiring an Attorney 2019 study, 6,300 legal consumers were asked how they found and chose an attorney. Among the ones who received a referral, only 41% said they hired the lawyer they were referred to. Another 32% chose someone else. The published figures don't say what the remaining 27% did.

What happened after a referral Share of referred consumers
Hired the recommended lawyer 41%
Chose someone else instead 32%
Not accounted for in the published figures 27%

I think that 41% surprises most attorneys, because a practice's own sense of its referrals is built entirely from the ones that worked. The family that got your name and quietly hired someone else never calls to tell you that. Neither does the one who meant to call and never did. Whatever your own hit rate actually is, your intake log has no way of showing it to you, because it only records the wins.

The decision moves faster than a referral pipeline assumes

A follow-up Martindale-Avvo survey, published in 2023, adds the timing piece. It found 55% of legal consumers decide within two weeks, and nearly 1 in 4 decide in three days or less. Half will contact between two and four attorneys before deciding. And nearly half of consumers looking to hire don't rely on a referral at all. They're searching and comparing on their own.

Put those two findings together and a referral starts to look like an entry ticket into a short, fast comparison, not a guaranteed engagement. If a family decides inside three days, whatever made them trust you had to already exist before that window opened. There usually isn't time to build it during the window itself.

Responding fast helps, but it isn't the whole fix

A 2025 Scorpion survey of 3,000 U.S. adults found 72% of potential legal clients said they'd move on if they didn't hear back within 24 hours. Scorpion sells marketing and intake tools to law firms, and this is a stated intention in a survey, not an observed outcome, so I wouldn't lean hard on the exact number. But it lines up with the speed in the Martindale-Avvo data. If someone is going to decide in three days, a slow callback uses up a meaningful share of that window on nothing.

Fixing response time matters. It also doesn't solve the earlier problem. A fast callback to someone who has never heard of you is still a cold introduction being compared against two or three others. Speed helps you compete in that comparison, it just doesn't get you out of it.

Build the relationship before the referral ever happens

The fix I'd put weight on is getting known directly to the families and the referral sources around them, well before anyone picks up the phone to make a referral. That means the CPAs and advisors who see a family's full financial picture first, and it means the families themselves, long before a sale, a diagnosis, or an inheritance makes the need urgent. By the time a name gets said out loud, you want it to already be a familiar one, not the first time that family has heard it.

What you're allowed to say directly to a prospective client, and how, still depends on your own state bar's version of the solicitation rules, and that's a question for your counsel, not me.

Common Questions

Why do so many attorney referrals go nowhere?

The best data I have on this is Martindale-Avvo's 2019 survey of 6,300 legal consumers. Among people who received a referral to a lawyer, only 41% said they hired the lawyer they were referred to, and 32% chose someone else. That survey covered general legal consumers, not estate or business matters specifically, and not wealthy families. My read is that a referral gets your name mentioned, and what happens between the mention and the decision is where most of the work still has to happen.

How fast do people actually decide on an attorney?

Faster than most referral systems plan for. A Martindale-Avvo survey published in 2023 found 55% of legal consumers decide within two weeks, and nearly 1 in 4 decide in three days or less. The same survey found nearly half of consumers looking to hire do not rely on a referral at all. Again, this is general consumer data, not a study of wealthy families.

Does responding faster to inquiries actually help?

It can't hurt, and the one figure I have suggests it matters more than most firms assume. A 2025 Scorpion survey of 3,000 U.S. adults found 72% of potential legal clients said they would move on if they didn't hear back within 24 hours. That's a stated intention in a survey run by a company that sells marketing to law firms, not an observed behavior, so I'd treat it as a reason to tighten response time rather than proof of an exact number.

Is any of this data specific to wealthy families or business owners?

No, and I want to be straight about that. I haven't found a study that measures how high-net-worth families or business owners choose an estate or business attorney specifically. Everything cited on this page covers general legal consumers. What I say about wealthy families and the strategy that follows is my own professional judgment, built from twenty years of working with attorneys and CPAs on exactly this problem.
