Why Do Estate Plans Get Signed But Never Funded?

A signature feels like the finish line. It isn't. A trust with the house still in the client's own name does nothing at the one moment it was built for, and I don't think that's mostly a forgetfulness problem.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Signing and funding are two different things a client has to do. One is a meeting. The other is weeks of paperwork nobody enjoys, done after the attorney's part feels finished.
  • I haven't found research that measures how often funding gets skipped, so this page is my own judgment from twenty years around this work, not a study.
  • There's a lot of money riding on this working. Cerulli projects $124 trillion will transfer through 2048, with high-net-worth and ultra-high-net-worth households accounting for more than half that volume from just 2% of households.
  • So I'd screen for follow-through before the signature, and build the relationship with the family's CPA so funding becomes a team project instead of a second ask from you alone.

Most attorneys I talk with measure their practice by signed engagements. That's the natural place to look, because it's the moment with a date on it, a fee attached, and a file that closes. I think it's the wrong finish line. A revocable trust that still has the family home, the brokerage account, and the business interest titled in the client's own name doesn't avoid probate, doesn't protect against incapacity, and doesn't do any of the things the client paid for. The trust is signed, but without the retitling, none of it actually works for the family yet.

Two different jobs, and the second one has no deadline

Getting a client to sign is one job: a meeting, a conversation about the family, a document to execute. Funding the trust is a different job entirely, retitling a deed, moving a brokerage account, updating beneficiary forms, and it's a job with no built-in deadline and nobody standing over the client's shoulder to make sure it gets done. Signing happens in your office while you're there. Funding happens weeks later, alone, squeezed in between everything else going on in the client's life.

I don't think most unfunded trusts are a sign the client didn't care. I think they're a sign the client never fully understood that the work wasn't done when the pen went down, because nobody made that second job as concrete as the first one.

I don't have a number for how often this happens

I haven't found research that puts a figure on what share of signed trusts go unfunded, or for how long. That's my own honest gap, not a case of the data being inconvenient. What I'm describing on this page comes from watching this pattern repeat across attorney clients over many years, not from a cited study, and I'd rather say that plainly than dress up a guess as a statistic.

Stage What it actually requires Where it tends to stall
Signing the plan One meeting, one signature, a fee paid Rarely. This part has a date and your attention
Funding the trust Retitling the house, the brokerage account, the business interest Often. No meeting, no deadline, no one watching
Updating beneficiary forms A form at the bank, the brokerage, the life policy Easy to forget because it isn't part of the signing meeting at all

Why this matters more as the wealth-transfer window gets closer

Cerulli projects that wealth transferred through 2048 will total $124 trillion, with $105 trillion going to heirs and $18 trillion to charity. High-net-worth and ultra-high-net-worth households account for more than half of that total, $62 trillion, while making up only 2% of all households. That's a projection, not a guarantee, and it says nothing about how many of those households have a plan or whether it's funded. What it tells me is the scale of what's riding on these documents actually working when they're needed, which is usually at death or incapacity, the two moments nobody can push back a few weeks to go finish the paperwork.

A pattern that rhymes, from a different vertical

I don't have estate-specific data on this, but there's a parallel from financial advising, with the caveat that it measures a different relationship entirely. Cerulli reported in 2023 that just one in five affluent investors use the same advisor as their parents, and that more than 90% of affluent investors who use their own advisor never seriously considered their parents' advisor at all. That's about advisor relationships surviving a generational handoff, not about trust funding, but the same shape shows up: something built carefully doesn't always work when it's actually needed, and that has nothing to do with whether it was built well in the first place.

Build the team before the signature

If funding happens alone, on a to-do list, after the attorney's part feels done, the fix is making sure the client isn't handling that task alone in the first place, not a sharper reminder letter. A CPA who already knows the client's accounts, and who has a reason to ask about them every filing season, is in a position to notice an unfunded trust long before you would. So is a financial advisor managing the brokerage account, who can walk the retitling through with the client directly.

That only works if you already know the CPA and the advisor as peers before the signing, long before there's anything to thank them for. I'd build that relationship on purpose, the same way I'd want a family to already know you before anything is urgent.

Common Questions

What does it mean for a trust to be funded?

It means the assets are actually retitled into the trust's name: the house deed, the brokerage account, the business interest. A signed trust document with the client's old accounts still in the client's own name doesn't do what the client paid for. The trust is signed, but without the retitling, none of it actually works for the family yet.

How common is it for estate plans to go unfunded?

I haven't found research that puts a number on it, so I can't give you one. What I see with my own attorney clients is that it happens often enough to matter, and it happens more with clients who were sold on urgency than with clients who came in already understanding why the paperwork alone isn't the point.

Should attorneys follow up on unfunded trusts?

Yes, and most do, but the follow-up call matters less than who you're calling. A client who understood the funding step before they signed answers that call differently than a client who thought signing was the whole job.

Does working with a client's CPA help get a trust funded?

In my experience, yes, because the CPA already sees the accounts that need retitling and has a reason to ask about them every year. I don't have a study that measures this. It's a pattern from watching attorney clients build those relationships on purpose.
