How CPAs Build Advisory Relationships With HNW Clients

The high-net-worth clients most CPAs want for advisory work are usually already on the list, filed under tax prep. The real question is who starts the bigger conversation first.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • Most CPAs already have the wealthy clients. They're the business owners, retirees, and executives already filed under compliance work, waiting for someone to start a bigger conversation.
  • A 2025 Journal of Financial Planning study found high-net-worth households are more likely to say their financial adviser already coordinates with their CPA. That conversation is happening around the CPA, not always through the CPA.
  • Referrals between CPAs, advisors, and attorneys are real but modest. Cerulli's 2025 data puts centers of influence at 13.9% of new advisor clients, well behind personal referrals.
  • The fix isn't more marketing to strangers. It's a deliberate, peer-to-peer relationship with the advisors and attorneys already circling the same households.
  • Being first into the advisory conversation beats being the most qualified for it. The advisor or attorney who starts that conversation usually ends up running it.

Most of the CPAs I talk with describe the same frustration. They want to move from compliance work into advisory work, the planning conversations that pay better and matter more to the client, but they feel like they need to go find a new book of wealthy clients to do it. That's backwards. The households are usually already sitting in the client files, filed under tax prep, waiting for someone to start a bigger conversation with them.

A 2025 study in the Journal of Financial Planning by Sommer and Lutter found that high-net-worth households were more likely than lower-wealth households to say their financial adviser already coordinates with their CPA. That doesn't mean the CPA started that coordination. It means the advisory conversation about that household's money is already happening, and the CPA's seat at the table gets decided by who moved first, not by who prepared the return.

Why busy season protects the wrong work

Compliance work has a deadline. Advisory development doesn't, so it loses every time the calendar gets tight, and the calendar is tight most of the year. Busy season eats January through April, the extension crunch eats the fall, and whatever's left over is where business development was supposed to live. For most CPAs, that's why it never actually happens. The idea isn't wrong. Nothing on the calendar ever forces it.

The advisors and attorneys serving the same households don't have that excuse in the same way. They're already having the planning conversation, on their own timeline, while the CPA is still waiting for a slower month that never arrives.

The advisory conversation is already happening, with or without you

The Sommer and Lutter study surveyed U.S. decision-makers with at least $250,000 in investable assets, including a group of 383 with $1 million or more, and found the wealthier group was more likely to report their adviser coordinates with CPAs and other outside advisers. The study didn't measure who initiated that coordination, and it didn't measure trust directly. But the practical read holds either way: by the time a household is wealthy enough to matter, somebody is already managing the full picture of their money. Often that's the advisor, even when the accountant has known the family longer.

That's not a knock on CPAs. It's a sequencing problem. The advisor has a natural reason to call the CPA once a year, usually around tax planning season, which means the advisor is the one who looks proactive. The CPA has the exact same reason to call the advisor, and almost never does.

Referrals count, but they're not the whole game

Cerulli's 2025 advisor metrics research reports that centers of influence, the category that includes CPAs and attorneys alongside other referral partners, produce 13.9% of new clients for the advisors who track it, a real number and a distant second to personal referrals. On the investor side, a 2023 Dynasty Connect survey found 23% of people who already had an advisor said a professional advisor, like an accountant or lawyer, was how they found them. Both numbers say the same thing from different angles: the CPA-to-advisor channel works, just not as the main engine.

A CPA whose growth plan depends on advisors and attorneys remembering to refer back doesn't control the pace of their own growth. Those relationships are worth keeping. They're just not something to build a whole plan on.

Becoming the CPA who's already in the room

The move that actually works is building direct, peer-to-peer relationships with the advisors and attorneys already serving households that look like the CPA's best advisory clients, not to get fed referrals, but to be visibly part of the team around that family before the big decision happens. Bottom line, this is the work I do: we're essentially creating new networks of the advisors, attorneys, and high-net-worth households who actually want to know you. We're not making the introductions ourselves, we create the network and generate the interest, then you take it from there directly.

Inside the existing client list, the same logic applies on a smaller scale. A CPA doesn't need to pitch advisory services to three hundred tax clients. A short list of the ones going through something big right now, selling a business, heading into retirement, sitting on stock that's gotten too concentrated, is worth a direct conversation framed as expanding what the CPA already does for them, not as a new service announcement. The approach stays peer to peer, so nothing about it reads as solicitation, and the daily consistency of building those relationships runs through Jarvis, my agentic operator, while the CPA does the work only they can do.

Common Questions

How do CPAs build advisory relationships with high-net-worth clients?

Usually not by finding new clients. The households are already in the client file, under tax prep. The work is turning an existing compliance relationship into an advisory one, and doing it before the client's financial advisor or attorney starts that conversation without you.

Can a CPA really sell advisory services to an existing tax client?

Treating it as a sale is the part that goes wrong. A direct pitch for a new service line reads to the client the way any pitch does, like an upsell. The CPAs who make the shift tend to expand a conversation that's already happening, a sale, a retirement, a concentrated stock position, rather than announce a new offering.

Why do financial advisors and attorneys get the advisory conversation instead of the CPA?

A 2025 Journal of Financial Planning study by Sommer and Lutter found high-net-worth households are more likely to say their financial adviser already coordinates with their CPA. That coordination usually starts from the advisor's side, because the advisor moved first. In practice, whoever starts that bigger conversation ends up owning it, whether or not they're the one who actually knows the numbers best.

Do referrals between CPAs, advisors, and attorneys actually produce new clients?

Some, but modestly. Cerulli's 2025 advisor data puts centers of influence, the category that includes CPAs and attorneys, at 13.9% of new client sources for advisors, second to personal referrals. It's real, but a CPA whose growth plan depends on advisors and attorneys remembering to refer back doesn't control the pace of their own growth.
