How Emerging Fund Managers Find Investors

The usual advice is to get in front of more investors. A 2014 paper in the Review of Financial Studies is why I'd put something else first. For a manager with a short record, time in the relationship does some of the work the record can't do yet.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • In one venture capital model, a new investor sees your returns and not much else. A 2014 paper in the Review of Financial Studies proposes that a venture firm's current investors learn about its skill while outside investors observe only returns, and it reports evidence consistent with that. An emerging manager has few returns to show and few existing investors to vouch for them.
  • Capital is concentrating. Preqin reports that just over half of the capital committed in 2024 went to the 100 biggest funds, compared with more than a third in 2021.
  • Family offices are a possible home, one office at a time. The label doesn't tell you whether your fund fits a given office's mandate, so I'd settle that investor by investor.
  • I'd start the relationship well before the raise, peer to peer, with investors who are likely within mandate, so they've had time to see how you think.

If you're raising a first or second fund right now, you already know the market is harder than your model assumed. Preqin's data shows where the money went. In 2024, just over half of committed capital went to the 100 biggest funds, compared with more than a third in 2021, and the number of funds that closed was less than half the 2021 count. Goldman Sachs Asset Management's 2025 Private Markets Diagnostic Survey says the shift toward fewer but larger commitments with existing managers remains intact.

The common response is to meet more investors. For an emerging manager, I think the harder problem is that a new investor has very little to judge you on, and that changes what I'd do about the raise.

In one venture capital model, new investors see returns and little else

In 2014, Hochberg, Ljungqvist and Vissing-Jorgensen published a paper in the Review of Financial Studies on performance persistence in venture capital. They propose a model in which, in their words, "Current investors learn about skill whereas outside investors observe only returns." In the model, outside investors read it as a bad sign when a firm's existing investors don't back the next fund, which gives existing LPs bargaining power over that fund. The authors report evidence consistent with the model.

A 2025 study by Shang and Leifeld in Social Network Analysis and Mining looked at who commits to whom. Using Preqin's records of commitments between 7,669 LPs and 7,153 GPs from 2016 to 2021, they found that past commitments between the same LP and GP significantly increase the chance of a future one.

All of that evidence has limits. The 2014 paper is a model of venture capital, with evidence its authors report as consistent with it. The 2025 study covers institutional LPs in private equity, real estate, private debt, natural resources and infrastructure, and it shows who ended up transacting without showing why. Preqin's concentration figure and Goldman's survey measure different things, and neither explains the other. None of these sources describes family offices or individual investors.

Now think about what that means if you're emerging. An established manager has returns for strangers to look at, and returning LPs who vouch for the firm by showing up again. You have a short record and nobody whose re-up sends a signal. I don't think more first meetings fix that, because a first meeting is the setting where an investor can see the least about you.

My reading: time is what stands in for the record

What follows is my reading of that paper, and the authors didn't say it. If investors learn about skill by being close to a manager over time, then the emerging manager's job is to let the right investors get close before there's anything to decide. Someone who has watched how you think for a year, and seen what you passed on and why, knows things about you that a deck can't carry. That's still less than an existing investor sees from inside a fund, and I don't claim the two are equal. It's the closest thing available to a manager with no existing investors yet.

That's why I keep coming back to one observation with every fund manager I work with. The raises I've seen go smoothly don't wait on introductions. The manager starts the relationship first, peer to peer, with allocators who were already within mandate and aligned with the thesis, long before anyone's asked to invest.

The clock argues for starting early too. Preqin's figures put the average time to close a fund across private capital at 25 months in July 2025, up from 16 months in 2020. That average isn't specific to first funds, but a relationship you open the week you launch gets very little time to mature before you need it.

Family office is a category, and fit gets settled one investor at a time

The other advice emerging managers hear is to go to family offices and wealthy individuals, because they decide for themselves. I've given a version of that advice. Some of them do move on their own judgment, and I've watched it happen. But I can't show you data that they decide faster or back new managers more readily, and the label doesn't tell you whether your fund fits. That gets settled one investor at a time. Is the fund within their mandate? Are they committing new money now? Can this person move it forward, and do they want to?

A relationship doesn't replace a record

The fair objection is that all of this is soft. LPs in the Goldman Sachs survey said they prioritize fees and terms, track records, and team stability. Kaplan and Schoar's 2005 study in the Journal of Finance found that better-performing partnerships are more likely to raise follow-on funds and to raise larger ones. That study is old and institutional, but I don't doubt the direction. Performance is a main driver of whether the next fund gets raised.

A long relationship won't carry a fund whose terms are off or whose team keeps changing. What it gives an investor is a way to see the things that sit behind a record before the record exists, like how you source and how you decide.

Where I'd put the effort

If I were emerging, I'd write down what my fund looks like from the investor's side of the table before I booked another meeting: the asset class, the stage, the check size, and the kind of investor whose mandate that plausibly fits. I'd start relationships with those people now, as a peer who's curious about what they're allocating toward, with no raise on the table. And I'd keep the group small enough that I could stay in real contact with each of them, because the point is for them to get to know me.

Todd, a friend running a fund, landed $35 million for his bourbon fund exactly this way. Pete, a client of mine, landed a $100 million investor building the relationship the same way. Nobody pitched anybody. Two raises aren't proof of a method, and I can't promise anyone a result like theirs. They're the cases that taught me to start early.

Common Questions

How long does it take to raise a first fund?

I don't have a verified figure for first funds specifically. Across all private capital, Preqin's data shows the average time to close a fund rose from 16 months in 2020 to 25 months in July 2025. That average isn't broken out by manager size or strategy, so treat it as the backdrop. In my experience, the managers who started building investor relationships well before launch had an easier time than the ones who started the day they began raising.

Should emerging managers hire a placement agent?

It can be the right call. Placement agents are licensed to place a fund with investors, which is a different job from anything I do. Whether one will take on a first or second fund is something you find out by asking, and I don't have data on how often they do. With or without one, I'd still want my own relationships with investors who are within mandate. I wrote more about that in How to Raise Capital Without a Placement Agent.

What do family offices look for in emerging managers?

It varies by office. The 2025 RBC and Campden Wealth report on North American family offices, which average US$2.0 billion in wealth including operating businesses, found that 88% have private market exposure and that direct private equity was the most popular asset class for new investment. Those are very large offices, and the report doesn't say what they look for in a newer manager. In my experience, the offices that do back one have had time to get to know the person, and the fund sat within what they were already trying to do.

Does cold outreach work for raising fund capital?

I haven't seen data showing that it fails, and I won't claim it does. iConnections, which sells introductions and conferences to allocators and managers, reports in its Global Allocator Report 2026 that nearly 80% of LPs discover new managers through their professional networks and a much smaller share through databases and cold outreach. That measures how institutional LPs find managers. It doesn't measure who they commit to. My own preference is a peer-to-peer relationship that begins before any raise, because it protects the scarcity a raise depends on and gives the investor time to know you.

If you're raising into this market and the math isn't working, grab time on my calendar. I read every one of these myself.
