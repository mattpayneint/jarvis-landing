How Do Estate Attorneys Find Families Ready to Implement an Estate Plan?

A referral tells you a family has money and a reason to need you. It says nothing about whether they're ready to sign anything, or how fast that window closes.

By Matthew Payne · Founder, Matthew Payne Consulting

The Short Answer

  • A referral and a hire are two different events. National research on legal consumers found that even after a referral, a large share still choose someone else.
  • The want-a-plan window moves fast once it opens. More than half of legal consumers who are ready to hire decide within two weeks, and a quarter decide within three days.
  • That speed is why waiting on the referral to arrive is risky. By the time most attorneys hear about the family, the decision clock may already be running.
  • The fix is having the relationship with the family, or with the people around them, already in place before the readiness window opens, not chasing more referral sources.
  • Referrals still matter. They just were never a guarantee on their own, and timing decides more of the outcome than most attorneys assume.

Most estate attorneys I talk to think their referral pipeline is the whole problem: not enough CPAs sending families, not enough advisors remembering their name in a given month. That's worth fixing. But there's a second problem hiding behind it, and it has little to do with how many referrals arrive. It's whether you're already known by the time the family is ready to sign anything, versus just willing to take a meeting.

What the research on legal hiring actually shows

Martindale-Avvo's 2023 consumer research found that nearly half of people looking to hire an attorney do not rely on a referral at all, and that 55 percent decide within two weeks, with nearly one in four deciding in three days or less. This is general consumer legal research, not a study of estate planning or wealthy households specifically, so it shouldn't be read as a precise estate-planning figure. But the pattern it shows, that the decision moves fast once it actually starts, lines up with what I see in estate work: families go from thinking about it to signing in a matter of days once something triggers the decision.

A referral is not a guarantee, even when it happens

Martindale-Avvo's 2019 study of attorney hiring found that among legal consumers who received a referral, only 41 percent actually hired the attorney who was recommended to them, and 32 percent ended up choosing someone else. A referral buys you trust, but the family still runs its own process: checking a website, maybe talking to one or two others, before they decide. The referral starts that process. It doesn't finish it. So the real goal is staying in the running and being known by the time the family starts that process, not getting referred more often.

The window closes faster than most practices are built for

Put the two findings together and the shape of the problem gets clearer. Families move through a real decision window, often two weeks or less, and a referral arriving right as that window opens is still only a coin flip on who gets hired. An attorney who shows up for the first time inside that two-week window, cold, is competing against whoever the family already half knows. The attorney who was already a known, trusted presence before the window opened skips that competition. They're the one the family calls first.

What this looks like in practice

The work is building relationships on both sides, with the families themselves and with the CPAs and advisors who tend to see a trigger coming before the family even names it. These are real conversations, as a peer, long before anything is urgent, with no pitch because there's nothing to pitch yet, just two people in the same world getting to know each other.
